Why this matters now

Every app you use, from your school’s timetable to the canteen’s online ordering, started as somebody’s problem, a plan, a budget and a great deal of testing. Projects rarely go wrong because the programming was too hard. They go wrong because nobody agreed what the system was for, nobody noticed a task running late, or nobody checked with the people who had to use it.

This focus area is where the course comes together: you plan and run a project, document it, choose how to build it, switch it on and judge whether it worked. It also holds tools that the HSC asks about again and again, such as Gantt charts, data flow diagrams and testing methods. On this page one made-up project, ClubHub, is used for every tool so you can see them working together.

Try this as you learn Think of a problem at your school or a club you belong to that a system could fix. Write it as a problem, not a solution, in two sentences, and add one way you would know it had been solved.

How this focus area is assessed

Two facts from NESA’s published pages (checked on 28 September 2026):

  • The HSC examination. Enterprise Computing has a single external examination, done on a computer: 80 marks in 2 hours 30 minutes including 10 minutes reading time, made up of objective-response items (about 20 marks) and short-answer items (about 60 marks). There is no submitted major project and no HSC practical exam, so this focus area is examined through questions on the tools and processes below, often with a scenario and the Course Specifications notations. Year 11 content is assumed knowledge. See NESA’s HSC examination specifications.
  • School-based assessment. The Year 12 school-based mark has two mandatory components, each weighted 50%: knowledge and understanding of course content, and knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content. Schools decide the number, type and weighting of tasks, within a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15% (for HSC courses the limit starts from Term 4, 2026). NESA publishes no compulsory project task, project weighting or project length for Enterprise Computing, so whether and how your school assesses a project is your school’s decision. Ask for your school’s assessment schedule. NESA also requires tasks to reflect a student’s own knowledge, skills and understanding rather than the use of generative AI. See school-based assessment and NESA’s notice on the take-home task limit.

Caveats. NESA’s Enterprise Computing pages do not state how school-based and examination marks are combined, and on 28 September 2026 their sample assessment schedules had not been published. These rules change, so confirm details with your teacher and NESA’s pages.

Managing and Documenting a Project

🎯 (EC-12-08, EC-12-09)

📌 Describe the tools and processes used to manage and document the development of an enterprise system

  • problem definition
  • time and resource management, including Gantt charts
  • an iterative approach
  • production process and technical skills
  • testing and evaluation
Our running example: ClubHub (a fictional project) Riverbend Community Sports Club (invented for these notes) has four courts, a clubhouse canteen and a few hundred members. Court bookings are taken on a paper sheet and by text message. The canteen coordinator counts stock by hand on Sunday nights and often finds out about shortages on Saturday morning. A team of four students (Ana, project manager and analyst; Ben, database; Chloe, interface; Dev, testing) is asked to build ClubHub, a booking and inventory system: members book a free court online, and the canteen coordinator records stock and gets a reorder list. Every tool on this page is applied to ClubHub, so you can watch one project move from idea to evaluation. The club, the people and all the figures are invented for teaching.

NESA defines project management as “the process of planning, organising, controlling resources, monitoring timelines and activities, and completing a project to achieve a goal that meets identified criteria” (NESA syllabus glossary). Project work in this focus area follows the design and production process: identifying and defining, researching and planning, producing and implementing, and testing and evaluating. Each stage has its own tools. The table shows where each one appears in these notes, so you can see the whole project before the detail.

Problem definition

A problem definition is a short, precise statement of the problem the project will solve. It is written first because every later decision, from which diagram to draw to whether the finished system counts as a success, is judged against it. A good one describes the problem, not the solution: “build an app” is a solution, whereas “members cannot see which courts are free, so double bookings happen” is a problem. It also says who is affected, what the project will and will not cover (its scope), the constraints (time, money, skills, technology) and measurable success criteria. NESA’s teaching advice says students identify “a real-world problem of interest and develop a solution”, so the definition must come from a real need, confirmed with the people who have it.

A model problem definition for ClubHub Problem. Riverbend Community Sports Club records court bookings on a paper sheet and by text message, and counts canteen stock by hand. Members cannot see which courts are free, the same court is sometimes booked twice, and the canteen runs short of popular items while over-ordering others.
Who is affected. Members, the club secretary, the canteen coordinator and the suppliers who receive orders.
Aim. Build an online system that lets members book a free court and lets the canteen coordinator record stock and produce a reorder list.
In scope. Court bookings, member fees status, canteen stock counts, reorder lists, booking and payment reports. Out of scope (this release). Online payments and the coaching timetable.
Constraints. Delivery within six weeks, a budget of up to $800 (all figures invented), a team of four students, and personal information about members that must be protected.
Success criteria. (1) The same court cannot be booked twice for the same time. (2) A member can make a booking in no more than four screens. (3) The canteen coordinator can produce a reorder list in one action. (4) Only authorised people can see member details.

Check a problem definition with four questions. Does it name a real problem rather than a product? Could a stranger tell who is affected? Can each success criterion be tested with a yes or no? Is the scope small enough to finish in the time available? If the answer to any is no, tighten it before you plan anything else.

Time and resource management, including Gantt charts

Time management means estimating how long each task will take, putting the tasks in a workable order and checking progress against the plan. Resource management means making sure the things the project needs are available when they are needed: people and their skills, hardware and software, data, money and each person’s hours. The main tool is the Gantt chart. NESA (Course Specifications, p.12) describes it as displaying “each of the component tasks in a proposed system development on an estimated timeline”, with self-explanatory task names, the time needed for each task and its dependent tasks, a dated time scale and marked milestones. Its second example adds team members and the percentage complete, and says charts “should be regularly updated during development to reflect actual versus estimated times for tasks”. The notation is explained in the Course Toolkit; here we apply it.

Reading a Gantt chart for problems is as important as drawing one. Ask which tasks are late or behind (compare the shaded share of a bar with how far along the time scale the status date is), which tasks depend on them, and which people are double-booked or idle. The longest chain of dependent tasks decides the earliest finish date, so a delay to any task on that chain moves the handover. A task off that chain has some slack. In ClubHub the chain runs database, stock screens, integration, testing, trial, handover, so Ben’s slip matters; a slip in Ana’s data flow diagram would not.

Exam focus: a Gantt chart to schedule and track a team’s allocated activities The 2025 HSC included a question about using a Gantt chart to schedule and track a team’s allocated activities (see the 2025 exam page). NESA’s marking guidelines have the exact wording; here is how to build a full answer, whether you are asked to draw the chart or to describe how it is used.
  1. Name the tasks with self-explanatory titles (“Design database”, not “Phase 2”) and group them under phases.
  2. Give each task a duration and dates on a dated time scale, not just “week 1, week 2”.
  3. Show dependencies with arrows from the end of one bar to the start of the task that needs it.
  4. Allocate every task to a named team member (or members) and check nobody is double-booked. This is the “allocated activities” part.
  5. Mark milestones as hollow diamonds, for example “Design approved”.
  6. Track progress: shade each bar to its percentage complete, compare it with where the status date says it should be, and record actual against estimated time. This is the “track” part.
  7. Update and act: revise dates when a task slips, say what the slip affects through the dependencies, and record the reason in the process diary.
A model answer in words, using ClubHub: “Ana interviews the committee (19–20 October) then writes the problem definition (21–22 October), which ends in the milestone Requirements approved on 23 October. Ben designs the database, Chloe designs the screens and Ana draws the data flow diagram in parallel from 26 October. At the end of 5 November the chart shows Build database 60% complete against 80% planned, so Ben is a day behind. Because Build stock screens depends on it, the schedule is revised and the reason recorded in the process diary.”

An iterative approach

NESA’s teaching advice describes project work as “an iterative process that builds on a systematic approach to problem identification, research, problem-solving, prototyping and evaluation”. Iterative means the team repeats a cycle of designing, building, testing and reviewing, improving the system a little each time, instead of expecting to get everything right in one pass. The design and production stages are not a one-way street: evidence from testing sends the team back to change the design, and a change to the design means the plan, the diagrams and the tests are updated too.

Iteration keeps a project honest because the client sees something early. In ClubHub the team did not wait until the end to find out whether members could use the booking screen:

Iteration is a habit that any development approach can use, and it is central to agile and prototyping (see Choosing a Development Approach). A common mistake is to iterate without a definition of “good enough”: return to your success criteria to decide when to stop.

Production process and technical skills

NESA’s glossary defines producing as “actively realising (making) designed solutions, using appropriate resources and means of production”. The production process is the sequence of steps that turns the design into a working system. For ClubHub it runs: set up the shared workspace and a backup routine; build the database tables from the schema and data dictionary; build screens from the approved storyboard; add validation so bad data is refused; connect the parts; and keep a written record of every change so that the work can be repeated, fixed or handed over.

Doing that takes technical skills, and a good team audits them early, because a missing skill is a risk. If nobody can do something, the team can train someone, share the task or (see approaches) outsource it.

Testing and evaluation

Testing and evaluation are not one step at the end. They run through the whole project, and they are how iteration decides what to change. Testing checks whether something behaves as expected. Evaluation, in NESA’s glossary wording, is to “make a judgement based on criteria; determine the value of”, so the criteria come from the problem definition: you evaluate ClubHub by asking whether it meets each success criterion.

The Changing Nature of Enterprise

🎯 (EC-12-06, EC-12-07)

📌 Explain the effect of the changing nature of enterprise on the development of projects

  • offshore development
  • working remotely
  • freelance work
  • enabling the growth of start-ups

Digital tools have changed where and by whom enterprise systems are built. Fast networks, cloud services and online collaboration tools mean a project team no longer has to sit in one room, work for one employer or even live in one country. NESA asks you to explain the effect of four changes on the development of projects. To explain is to give causes and effects, so for each change the notes below say what it is, how it changes the way a project is planned and built, the benefits and the risks, and how a team manages them. In ClubHub’s case the team is local, so each change is illustrated by asking “what if the club had done it this way instead?”.

Offshore development

Offshore development means having software or systems built by a team in another country. It is not the same as outsourcing, which means paying an outside organisation to do the work wherever it is; a company can offshore work to its own overseas office, and it can outsource to a firm down the road. Enterprises offshore to reach skills that are scarce at home, to lower the cost of development and to keep work going across time zones.

Effect on the project. The team must plan for distance. A large time difference leaves few overlapping working hours, so meetings are scheduled, decisions can take a day longer and the Gantt chart has to allow for the delay. Requirements must be written clearly enough to be understood without a conversation, which pushes teams towards detailed specifications, prototypes and written acceptance tests. Differences in language and culture can cause misunderstandings that show up as defects late in the project.

Benefits: access to specialist skills, potentially lower costs, extended working hours and more capacity. Risks: communication delays, quality that is harder to check, dependence on one supplier, and legal and security issues. Under the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), an Australian organisation that discloses personal information to an overseas recipient must generally take reasonable steps to ensure the recipient does not breach the Australian Privacy Principles (APP 8, cross-border disclosure; see the OAIC guidelines), so where member data is stored and who can see it must be decided early. Ownership of the code, confidentiality and how disputes are resolved belong in the contract.

Managing it: agree overlap hours, use a shared plan and task board, split the work into small deliverables with acceptance tests, review code regularly and keep documentation up to date so knowledge is not locked up in another country.

Working remotely

Working remotely means team members work from home or another place away from a shared office, connected by video meetings, messaging, cloud storage and shared documents. Remote working is possible because the project’s materials (documents, diagrams, code, test results) are digital and can be shared online, as the Networking Systems notes explain for connectivity and work.

Effect on the project. The team can recruit from a wider area, and members save travel time, but the plan has to replace the conversations that happen by chance in an office. Teams write down decisions, keep a shared Gantt chart and process diary, hold short regular check-ins and rely on tools such as version control so that two people do not overwrite each other’s work. Progress is judged by finished tasks rather than by who is seen working, so tasks need to be small and clearly defined.

Benefits: a wider pool of talent, flexibility, lower office costs and continuity when people cannot travel. Risks: weaker communication, isolation and unclear expectations; and security, because company data now sits on home networks and personal devices. Managing this uses the controls in Cyber Risk Management: multi-factor authentication, encrypted connections, access only to the data a role needs, and clear rules about which tools may hold personal information. A ClubHub example: if Ben works from home on the member table, he should use test data, not real members’ details.

Freelance work

Freelance work is work done by a self-employed person who takes on tasks or projects for different clients, rather than being an employee. Freelancers are used for particular pieces of work such as an interface design, a database build or testing.

Effect on the project. A team can add a missing skill for exactly as long as it is needed, and the project plan can be built around specialist tasks. The team must also plan the handover: a freelancer’s knowledge leaves when the contract ends, so the work has to be documented and the deliverables clearly defined (what will be delivered, when, to what standard, and for what payment, often by milestone). Availability is a scheduling risk, because a freelancer may have other clients.

Benefits: flexible cost, specialist skills, fast start. Risks: availability, uneven quality, dependence on one person, and ownership of the work. In Australian law the creator of a work is generally its first owner under the Copyright Act 1968, so a client who wants to own or freely modify a freelancer’s code needs the contract to say so in writing, and should get legal advice on the wording. For ClubHub, the club might hire a freelance graphic designer for the logo; the agreement should state that the club may use and change it.

Enabling the growth of start-ups

A start-up is a new enterprise, usually built around an idea for a product or service, that aims to grow quickly. Cloud computing, software as a service, open-source software and app stores have lowered what it costs to begin, because a start-up can rent computing power by the month instead of buying servers, and can add offshore, remote and freelance help without a large payroll. The Networking Systems notes discuss what makes a start-up succeed in Start-ups and success.

Effect on the project. Start-up projects tend to be short, iterative and focused on getting something in front of users quickly. A common tactic is to build a minimum viable product (MVP), the simplest version that solves the core problem and lets the founders learn from real users, then improve it in cycles. Requirements change often, so agile approaches and prototypes are popular, and money is limited, so the budget and the time-and-resource plan are tighter.

Benefits: lower entry costs, speed and the ability to test an idea before committing much money. Risks: running out of money, depending on other companies’ platforms (a price rise or a change of terms affects the whole business), neglecting security and privacy because of pressure to launch, and difficulty scaling a quickly built system. A ClubHub example: a start-up could turn ClubHub into a service for many clubs; its first job would be a minimum viable product for one, using hosted cloud services rather than buying hardware.

Online collaboration

Online collaboration tools let a team share and work on project materials without being in the same place. The main kinds are shared document and file storage (with version history so an earlier copy can be restored), messaging and video meetings, task boards that show each task as a card moving from “to do” to “done”, and version control for code and other files that lets several people change the same project and merge their work. NESA defines collaborate as “working with others towards a shared goal, through a variety of modes of communication”, and these tools are those modes.

Criteria for choosing. Can everyone reach it, including on a phone? Does it keep version history? Can access be limited by role? Where is the data stored and who can read it? What does it cost? ClubHub’s team chose a shared folder with version history, a task board for weekly tasks and a messaging group for quick questions. Judgement: online collaboration is essential, but only with rules: agreed file names, one owner per document, and no real member data in chat messages.

Time/task action plans

A time/task action plan is a short list, usually for a week or a work session, of the tasks to be done, who is responsible, when each is due and its status. It sits beneath the Gantt chart: the Gantt chart shows the whole project and its dependencies, the action plan says what each person does this week. It is quick to write and easy to review at a check-in.

Use it with the Gantt chart, not instead of it. Judgement: a small team gets the most from a Gantt chart for the whole project and an action plan for each week.

Process diary, including ongoing evaluation

A process diary (or log book) documents the progress of the project as it happens. NESA (Course Specifications, p.13) says entries made by team members at regular intervals should include the date, the person making the entry, progress since the last entry, tasks achieved, stumbling blocks or issues and how they were managed, possible approaches for upcoming tasks, reflective comments and resources used. The layout of each item is in the Course Toolkit. Ongoing evaluation is the habit of judging your work against your success criteria as you go rather than only at the end: the reflective comment in each entry is where you do it. Ongoing evaluation is why a diary is more than a record; it changes what the team does next.

A model process diary entry (ClubHub) Date: Thursday 5 November 2026. Person: Ben. Progress since last entry (Monday): the Members, Courts and Bookings tables are built and linked; the Stock and Suppliers tables have their fields but are not yet linked. Tasks achieved: created three tables from the data dictionary and loaded 40 invented test members. Stumbling blocks: importing the club’s stock list failed because some item names had extra spaces and two items appeared twice. I removed the spaces in the spreadsheet first, then checked the duplicates with the canteen coordinator before merging them. Possible approaches for next tasks: either check for double bookings in the screen code or make the database refuse a second booking for the same court and time; I prefer the database rule because it works however a booking is entered. Reflective comments: the import took a day longer than planned because I did not look at the real data during research; next time we inspect real data before we estimate. I am about one day behind on the Gantt chart and have told Ana so the stock screens can be rescheduled. Resources used: database software, a copy of the stock spreadsheet, advice from the canteen coordinator.

Judgement: the diary is the cheapest and most useful tool on the list, because it records the reasons behind changes to the Gantt chart. Its weakness is that it is only as good as its entries: a diary written the night before it is due is not ongoing evaluation.

Budget

A budget is a plan of the money a project will need and where it will be spent. It answers a feasibility question (“can we afford this?”) and, once the project is running, a control question (“are we within what we planned?”). List every cost you can identify, estimate each one, add a contingency (an amount held back for costs you did not foresee), and compare the total with the money available. System flowcharts

A system flowchart shows the main processes and devices in a system and how data moves between them. It answers “what is this system made of?” at the level of hardware and stored data. The NESA symbols (Course Specifications p.7) are the paper document, process, direct access storage, online display, manual operation, telecommunications link, online input, magnetic tape and cloud; the Toolkit has the key. Arrows show the direction of data, not the order of steps, and no flowchart symbols (terminator, decision) are mixed in.

Data flow diagrams

A data flow diagram (DFD) shows what data moves between the people outside a system, the processes inside it and the places data is stored. It says nothing about screens or hardware, which is why clients understand it. NESA’s symbols (Course Specifications pp.4–5) are the circle (process), open-ended rectangle (data store), closed near-square rectangle (external entity) and labelled curved arrow (data flow); see the Toolkit. A Level 0 diagram is an overview of the entire system: one process, all the external entities and no data stores.

How to derive it. (1) List who or what supplies data to the system or receives data from it: these are the external entities (members, the club secretary, the canteen coordinator, suppliers). (2) Write the single system process in the middle. (3) For each entity, list what it sends in and what it gets out, and name each flow with a noun phrase describing the data (“Booking request”, not “Book”). (4) Give every arrow a head and a label, and draw two-way exchanges as two separate curved arrows.

Decision trees

A decision tree shows how a series of yes/no or multiple-choice questions leads to an outcome. NESA’s examples (Course Specifications p.8) use rectangles joined by labelled branches, and each path ends in a final action; see the Toolkit. A decision tree is the clearest way to record a business rule and to check it is complete. To build one: write the outcomes the system can produce, work out the questions that separate them, put the most important question at the top and label every branch. Then test every path with an example.

Choosing between the tools

A small team rarely uses every tool at full strength. The choice depends on the question being asked, so match the tool to the question rather than drawing every diagram.

Computational thinking in ClubHub

The rule “a court cannot be booked twice” looks simple until a computer has to check it. Computational thinking turns it into steps. Decomposition: split booking into finding the court, reading its existing bookings, comparing times and saving the booking. Pattern recognition: two bookings clash whenever one starts before the other ends and ends after the other starts. Abstraction: ignore who the member is and what the court looks like, and keep only court, start time and end time. Algorithm: for each existing booking on this court, if the new start is before its end and the new end is after its start, refuse; otherwise save. That algorithm can be drawn as a flowchart or decision tree and then tested with clashing and non-clashing times (see test data).

Design thinking in ClubHub

Design thinking starts with people. A common version of the process is: empathise (interview and observe members, the secretary and the canteen coordinator), define the problem in their terms, ideate possible solutions, prototype the best ones cheaply, and test with users, then repeat. It also weighs the effects of the design. ClubHub’s economic impact is the cost of hosting against the time saved; its environmental impact is less paper; its social impact includes members who are not comfortable online, which is why a phone booking option is kept. The Interactive Media notes describe the process in more detail in Design Thinking and UX.

Systems thinking in ClubHub

Systems thinking looks at the whole system and the connections between its parts. NESA’s glossary defines an enterprise system as “the combination of digital systems, people and processes that collect, manage and analyse data”, so a system is more than software. Adding online booking has consequences elsewhere: more games played means more canteen sales, which means stock runs out sooner, which means the reorder list matters more. That chain is a feedback loop between two parts of the system that were designed as separate modules. Systems thinking also asks who is affected outside the system (suppliers, members without internet access) and which data is shared between parts, so the Bookings and Stock tables must use the same court and member identifiers.

Try this The club decides members should be able to cancel a booking. Write one sentence for each type of thinking: what would you decompose, whom would you ask, and what else in the system changes?
A model answer

Computational: break cancelling into finding the booking, checking the member owns it, checking how long before the game it is (a cancellation rule), removing it and freeing the court, then write the steps as an algorithm or decision tree. Design: ask members and the secretary what they expect to happen (a refund? a confirmation message?), draw a prototype cancel screen and test it. Systems: a freed court becomes bookable again, the fees or payment report changes, the waiting list may need a new alert, and the canteen may expect fewer players.

Exam focus: keep the three skills separate A “describe how … skills are used” question rewards precision. For each skill: name it, give its purpose in a phrase, then show a specific use in the scenario. Marks are lost by giving the same example for all three, by describing design thinking as “designing the screens” without users or testing, and by describing systems thinking as “thinking about the whole thing” without naming components and their connections. A useful check: computational thinking makes something a computer can do, design thinking checks it suits the people, and systems thinking checks what it affects.

Designing for ease of operation and maintenance

Ease of operation is how easily the people who use the system every day can do their jobs with it. It comes from clear screens, sensible defaults, helpful error messages, few steps for common tasks, and accessibility: strong colour contrast, text that can be enlarged and controls big enough to touch (the W3C’s WCAG quick reference lists the standards). For ClubHub the canteen coordinator will use a tablet in a busy kitchen, so buttons are large and the stock list is sorted by the items that run out most often.

Ease of maintenance is how easily the system can be kept working and improved after the team has moved on. Design for it by building in separate modules, using meaningful names for tables, fields and screens, keeping a data dictionary and diagrams up to date, keeping settings (fees, court names, reorder levels) in data rather than in code, and writing maintenance documentation someone else can follow. Three kinds of maintenance are commonly distinguished (the terms are widely used in industry, though NESA does not name them): corrective (fixing faults), adaptive (changing the system because its environment changed, such as a new fee structure) and perfective (improving it, such as adding a reminder email). A system designed for maintenance makes all three cheaper.

Negotiating user/client needs and wants

A need is something the system must do for the project to be worth building. A want is something people would like but can live without. Tools for Requirements and Limitations

🎯 (EC-12-08, EC-12-10)

📌 Apply tools to inform the requirements and limitations of an enterprise system

  • interviews
  • surveys

  • prototypes

Before a system is designed, the team needs to know what it must do (its requirements) and what will hold it back (its limitations). Requirements are the functions and qualities the users and client need, such as “a member can see which courts are free”. Interviews

An interview is a planned conversation to learn how something works now and what people need from a new system. Prepare by deciding the purpose, choosing who to interview (the people who will use the system and the person who decides), writing questions, and asking permission to take notes or record. Use open questions that invite a description (“Walk me through…”) and follow-ups (“Can you show me?”, “Why is that?”). Prototypes

NESA defines a prototype as “a trial product or model built to test an idea or process to inform further design development”, tested by users and systems analysts. Prototypes let the team find out whether the requirements were understood before anything expensive is built. A low-fidelity prototype is drawn on paper or as plain wireframes and is quick to change; a high-fidelity or clickable prototype looks and behaves more like the finished system and tests how it feels to use. The Interactive Media notes compare the fidelity levels.

ClubHub’s first prototype was three sheets of paper. Two members were asked to “book a court for Saturday at 10”, while a team member acted as the computer by swapping the paper screens. Both tried to choose the date before the court, which is why the storyboard was redrawn (see the iterations table in Managing and Documenting a Project). Record what users did, not just what they said, and be ready to be wrong.

Presentations of research results

A presentation of research results shares the findings with the client and team so that they can agree on the requirements and next steps. Plan it for the audience: the club committee wants to know the problem, what was found, what the options cost and what you recommend, not every survey response. A clear structure is: the problem, how we found out, what we found (three or four charts or tables), what we recommend (with cost and time), and the decision we need. Use one message per slide, label axes and units, do not truncate an axis to exaggerate a difference, and keep detail in the report. The presentation is also a control point: when the committee approves it, the “Requirements approved” milestone can be ticked.

Choosing a Development Approach

🎯 (EC-12-08, EC-12-09)

📌 Explore and apply the most suitable development approach to develop, modify and implement an enterprise system

  • waterfall (structured)
  • agile
  • prototyping
  • end-user
  • outsourcing

A development approach is the way a team organises the work of building a system: in what order the stages happen, how much the client is involved and how changes are handled. There is no single best approach. To explore and apply them is to understand how each one works, compare them against a scenario’s needs and then use the one that fits, whether you are developing a new system, modifying an existing one or implementing it. NESA lists five: waterfall (structured), agile, prototyping, end-user and outsourcing.

The five approaches

Waterfall (structured). The stages (requirements, design, development, testing, installation, maintenance) run in order, and each is finished and signed off before the next begins. It relies on a complete specification at the start. Agile. The system is built in short cycles (often called sprints, of one to four weeks) that each produce a working increment; the client reviews it, and the next cycle is planned from the feedback. Prototyping. Early models are built and refined with users until the design is right, and then the final system is built (or the prototype is evolved into it). End-user development. The people who will use the system build it themselves, typically with spreadsheets, database packages or no-code tools, without a professional developer. Outsourcing. The work is contracted to an outside organisation that delivers the system, or part of it, to an agreed specification.

Planning Implementation

🎯 (EC-12-08, EC-12-09, EC-12-10)

📌 Develop an implementation plan and test its feasibility for an enterprise computing system

  • design thinking
  • thinking and design tools, including storyboards, Gantt chart, decision tree
  • risk analysis
  • hardware and software integration
  • training
  • preferred system implementation method
  • methodology for testing the system

An implementation plan sets out how a system will be put into use: what will be done, in what order, by whom, with what resources and risks, how people will be prepared, and how the team will know it works. Testing its feasibility means checking, before committing time and money, that the plan can actually succeed. Every part of the plan uses tools from earlier in these notes, so this section shows them working together, again with ClubHub.

Design thinking

NESA’s glossary describes design thinking as a process in which “a need or opportunity is identified and a design solution is developed”, with “the consideration of economic, environmental and social impacts” at its core, used “when trying to understand a problem, generate ideas and refine a design based on evaluation and testing”. In an implementation plan design thinking is the habit of asking how the plan will work for people, not just what the technical steps are. Testing the plan with the people who will live with it (the secretary, the canteen volunteers, a sample of members) finds problems while they are cheap to fix. The impacts should be written down.

Thinking and design tools, including storyboards, Gantt chart, decision tree

Three tools carry most of an implementation plan. A storyboard shows the screens in order so that the client and users can agree on the interface, and it is the plan for the build. NESA’s storyboard (Course Specifications p.9) has a title, a Help button, navigation buttons with the current page highlighted, a content panel and a dot-and-arrow for a button that opens another screen; see the Toolkit. A Gantt chart schedules the implementation tasks (installing hardware, importing data, training, testing, go-live) with owners and milestones; the ClubHub chart in Managing and Documenting a Project covers the build, and an implementation chart would extend it. A decision tree records rules, and, as shown later in this section, can also guide the choice of implementation method.

Risk analysis

Risk analysis identifies what could go wrong, judges how likely and how serious each problem would be, and decides what to do about it. The results go into a risk register. The steps are: (1) identify risks by asking the team, users and the client; (2) assess each by likelihood and impact (here Low = 1, Medium = 2, High = 3, multiplied to give a rating: 1–2 low, 3–4 medium, 6–9 high); (3) respond, by avoiding the risk, reducing it, transferring it (for example to a supplier or through insurance) or accepting it; and (4) monitor, with an owner for each risk and regular review. The Course Specifications do not prescribe a risk matrix, so a simple scale like this is acceptable if it is applied consistently. The Cybersecurity notes show the same idea for security risks.

Hardware and software integration

Integration is making the new hardware and software work together, and with what the organisation already has. Training

Training prepares the people who will use and support the system so that they can do their jobs on the first day. Different groups need different things, and the best methods are matched to the group. Untrained users are one of the most common reasons a technically successful system fails, because they avoid it, make errors or ask the same questions again and again. Training should happen shortly before go-live, use the real system with practice data, and be evaluated afterwards by watching people complete tasks. Preferred system implementation method

NESA’s Course Specifications (p.14) name four methods: direct, parallel, pilot and phased. The glossary describes system implementation as “the implementation method applied when introducing a new or redesigned enterprise system”. The Toolkit defines each one. The animation compares them for a generic four-office organisation.

ClubHub’s choice. A booking failure would not be dangerous, and it would cost some goodwill; and the system splits into two modules used by different people. The tree leads to phased: the stock module goes live first, used only by the canteen coordinator, and the booking module goes live three weeks later once the team has learned from the first. During the first fortnight of booking, the paper sheet stays available as a fallback, which borrows a short parallel run for the module that affects the most people. The reasons: a fault in the stock module affects one person, and by the time booking goes live the team has a working, tested system and a trained secretary. The trade-off is that the club runs two ways of working for a while and full benefits arrive later than with direct implementation. Rejected: direct implementation, because a booking fault would reach every member on the first day; and full parallel running, because the secretary would enter every booking twice.

Verifying and Validating a System

“Verify” and “validate” are often used as if they meant the same thing, and in this dot point they do not. A team that only verifies can build exactly what the specification says and still fail, because the specification was wrong. A team that only validates can please the users in a demonstration and still ship a system full of faults. Both are needed.

Notice that the words are also used for data. To validate data is to check that it is reasonable and allowed before it is accepted (a range, type, presence or format check). To verify data is to check that it was entered or copied correctly, for example by entering it twice or comparing it with the source. The Data Visualisation notes use these meanings. NESA’s dot point asks you to verify and validate an enterprise computing system, so a system-level answer is usually wanted, but read each question’s context and use data-level examples where the question is about data entry.

Evaluating test data

A test is only as good as its data. Evaluating test data means two things: judging whether the set of test data is good enough to give confidence (it is the right data), and then judging what the results say about the system. Good test data covers three kinds of values. Normal data is typical, valid data the system should accept. Boundary data is on and next to each limit: for a rule of 30 to 120 minutes, test 30 and 29, and 120 and 121. Erroneous data is data the system must reject: the wrong type, a negative number or a blank. The expected result for every test is worked out before testing, from the requirements, so you are checking the system rather than believing it. Trialling the operation and maintenance documentation

Documentation is part of the system: it is how people run it and keep it running. Operation documentation (often a user guide) explains how to do each task, what the messages mean and who to contact. Maintenance documentation is written for the people who look after the system: how it is built (diagrams, data dictionary, schema), how it is configured, how backups are taken and restored, how to apply updates and a log of changes. A guide is only good if a person can follow it, so it has to be trialled: give it to someone who was not involved in building the system, ask them to complete real tasks using only the documents, watch without helping, and note where they hesitate, get stuck or make mistakes. Then fix the documents and trial again.

Modifying designs to improve functionality

Testing and impact reviews produce evidence that the design can be better. Modifying a design is a controlled process, not a quick fix: identify the cause, decide the change against the success criteria, update the design documents first (storyboard, data flow diagram, decision tree, data dictionary), make the change, retest the change and the features around it, and record it in the process diary and a change log.
