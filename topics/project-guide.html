Why this matters now

A project is judged by more than the thing you build. The plan you made, the evidence that you followed it, the way you handled a client who changed their mind and the honesty of your evaluation all count. Students who skip the planning usually discover the problem in the last two weeks, when there is no time left to fix it.

This guide is the practical companion to the Enterprise Project syllabus page and the Course Toolkit. Those pages explain the theory and the notation NESA expects. This one gives you checklists, a chooser for picking a development approach, and templates you can copy into your own documents. It follows NESA’s four stages of the design and production process, in order.

Try this as you learn Think of a group task that went wrong, such as a group assignment or a club event. At which of the four stages (identify, plan, produce, test) did the trouble really start?

How this page relates to the rest of the site The syllabus wording and the NESA Course Specifications notation live on the Enterprise Project page and in the Course Toolkit; this guide links to them rather than repeating them. Where we describe a technique that NESA does not name (the critical path, MoSCoW, the four feasibility checks, risk registers), it is labelled as a common technique. Assessment facts in Part 7 come only from NESA pages checked on 28 September 2026. Your school’s own schedule always comes first.
Part 1 The project life cycle at a glance

NESA organises the Enterprise Project around four stages: identifying and defining, researching and planning, producing and implementing, and testing and evaluating. Every project you will ever meet, from a school canteen app to a hospital records system, moves through them, usually more than once.

Ask yourself If you had to hand your project to another student halfway through, which documents would they need in order to carry on?

The Four Stages at a Glance

Design and production process Identifying and defining, researching and planning, producing and implementing, testing and evaluating

The stages and what each one leaves behind

The four stage names are NESA’s subheadings for the Year 12 Enterprise Project. Each stage ends with something you can point to. If you cannot point to it, the stage is not finished, however busy you have been. The theory for each stage is on the Enterprise Project page; this guide is about doing the work.

Stage summary table

StageThe question it answersMain toolsTypical evidence
1. Identifying and definingWhat is the problem, for whom, and how will we know it is solved?Problem definition, interview or survey, success criteriaSigned-off problem definition, interview notes, list of stakeholders
2. Researching and planningWhat will we build, in what order, with what time, money and risks?Gantt chart, action plan, budget, DFD, system flowchart, storyboard, risk registerDated plan, design documents, requirements list, feasibility notes
3. Producing and implementingAre we building what we planned, and how will the client start using it?Development approach, prototype, implementation plan, process diaryWorking versions, diary entries, version history, training and documentation
4. Testing and evaluatingDoes it work, and does it meet the criteria we set?Test plan, test data, client feedback, evaluation tableTest results, evaluation against criteria, maintenance notes, reflection

Why the cycle repeats

NESA lists an iterative approach as part of managing a project. In practice that means you do not finish stage 1 and never look at it again. A client interview in stage 1 gives you a problem definition; a prototype in stage 3 shows the client something new; they change their mind; and you go back to update the requirements and the plan. This is normal, and a record of it in your process diary is strong evidence that you managed the project rather than just built it.

Work at the right size NESA gives the Enterprise Project focus area 30 indicative hours in Year 12 (“indicative” is NESA’s word: it is a guide, not a fixed limit). Choose a problem you can genuinely finish. A small system that is defined, planned, tested and evaluated well beats an ambitious one that is half built.

Part 2 Choosing a development approach

NESA names five development approaches: waterfall (structured), agile, prototyping, end-user and outsourcing. No approach is best in general. The right one depends on your client, your requirements, your team, your time and your budget, and a good project report says which of those facts drove the choice.

Ask yourself If your client could only be reached at the start and the end of the project, which approach would worry you most, and why?

The Five Approaches Compared

Explore and apply How each approach works, when it fits and when it goes wrong

Side by side

ApproachHow it worksFits best whenGoes wrong when
Waterfall (structured)Stages are completed in order: requirements, design, development, testing, installation, maintenance. Each is documented and signed off before the next begins.Requirements are clear and stable, the client is hard to reach, and a fixed date and scope must be met.The client changes their mind late. A working system may appear only near the end, so misunderstandings are found late too.
AgileShort cycles (sprints in Scrum) each plan, build, test and review a small working piece. The client gives feedback every cycle and work is taken from a prioritised backlog.Requirements will evolve, the client can give frequent feedback and the team can adapt the plan.The client is rarely available, the scope and price must be fixed in advance, or the team drops the planning and records altogether.
PrototypingA quick, cut-down version is built early so users can react to it, then refined or replaced.Nobody can describe what they want until they see something, especially for screens and workflows.The prototype is mistaken for the finished product, or the client keeps asking for “one more version” and the project never ends.
End-user developmentThe people who will use the system build or adapt it themselves, using accessible tools such as spreadsheets or simple app builders.The need is small and specific, the users know their own process best and the budget is tight.The system grows beyond what the tool can safely do, the builder leaves and nobody understands it, or data security and backups are ignored.
OutsourcingAnother organisation, sometimes in another country, is paid to build part or all of the system to an agreed specification.You lack a key skill, there is budget, and the requirements can be written down clearly.The specification is vague, communication is slow, or the client loses control of quality, data and the schedule.

Waterfall and agile in motion

Combining approaches

Real projects rarely use one approach in pure form. A common pattern is a short waterfall-style start (define the problem and agree the scope), agile sprints for the build, and prototyping inside the sprints to test screens with the client. A school project might be end-user built (the club secretary’s spreadsheet) with outsourced help for one difficult feature. Whatever you choose, name it, give the reasons drawn from your own project and state the drawback you are accepting and how you will manage it.

Common mistakes Choosing agile because it sounds modern, with no client feedback in the plan. Choosing waterfall and then having no written requirements to sign off. Treating a prototype as the finished system. Describing an approach in general terms without a single reason from your own project.
Exam tip NESA’s syllabus asks you to “explore and apply the most suitable development approach”. In an exam scenario, name one approach, give two reasons taken from the scenario (client availability, stability of requirements, budget, skills, deadline) and acknowledge one weakness. Question 24 of the 2025 HSC asked about offshore development and freelance work, which links to outsourcing and end-user development in this list.
Part 3 Planning tools in practice

The Toolkit shows NESA’s notation for a Gantt chart and a process diary. This part is about using them: how to build a schedule that holds up, how to break the next fortnight into tasks, how to write diary entries that count as evidence, and how to put a price on a project.

Ask yourself If you lost a week to illness, which tasks in your plan could slip without moving your final date, and which could not?

Gantt Chart Walkthrough

Time and resource management Dependencies, milestones, the critical path and percentage complete

Start from the tasks, not the chart

The chart is the last step. First list the tasks in words a stranger would understand (“Interview the canteen manager”, not “Phase 1”), estimate each in days, and ask of every task: what must be finished before this can start? The answers are your dependencies. NESA’s required elements are on the Toolkit Gantt page: self-explanatory task names, estimated times, clearly shown dependencies, a dated time scale and marked milestones. The example below groups a library booking app project under NESA’s four stage names.

The critical path, explained simply

Some tasks can run at the same time; some cannot. Follow the dependencies from the first task to the last and add up the days along each route. The longest route decides the earliest possible finish date. That route is called the critical path: a delay to any task on it delays the whole project. Tasks off the path have float, spare days they can slip without moving the end date. (The critical path is a common project-management technique. NESA’s Course Specifications do not name it, but it explains why some delays matter and others do not.)

Answers

Cutting E to 2 days gives 2 + 5 + 6 + 2 = 15 days. If C grows to 8 days, the route A, C, D, E becomes 2 + 8 + 6 + 3 = 19 days and is still critical, while B (3 days) gets even more float. B never becomes critical unless it grows past the length of C.

Milestones and percentage complete

  • Milestones are decision or delivery points: “Requirements approved”, “Prototype shown to client”, “Testing complete”. Add one at the end of each stage and treat it as a checkpoint: are we really done?
  • Percentage complete shows how much of a task is finished. Be honest: count finished work, not time spent. A task is 80% complete when 80% of the work is done, not when 80% of the days have passed.
  • Actual versus estimated: NESA says the chart should be updated during development. Keep the original plan and note the actual dates beside it. The differences are the material for your diary reflections and your evaluation.

Keeping the chart alive

  1. Update the chart at the same time each week, and note the date of the update.
  2. Move any task that has slipped, then check whether the critical path changed.
  3. If the end date moves, decide what to do: drop a low-priority requirement (see MoSCoW), add time, or ask the client.
  4. Record the reason in your process diary.
Common mistakes Estimating every task at “a week”. Leaving out testing, training and documentation, which always take longer than expected. Planning to work every day with no spare time. Never updating the chart, so it shows the plan and not what happened.
Exam tip Question 22 of the 2025 HSC asked about project management tools. When a question gives you tasks and durations, work out the dependencies first, then place the bars, mark a milestone and say who is doing each task. Note the longest chain: it explains which delays matter.

Time/Task Action Plans

Researching and planning Turning the Gantt chart into what each person does this week

A Gantt chart shows the whole project. An action plan is the short-range view: the tasks for the next week or two, who owns each, when it is due and whether it is done. Keep it beside your Gantt chart, and rewrite it each week from the chart. One task, one owner, one date: if two people own a task, nobody does.

Template: weekly action plan
TaskOwnerDueDepends onStatusNotes
Draw Level 0 DFD for canteen orderingAnaTue 4 AugInterview notesIn progressCheck flow labels with the manager
Build the Menu and Orders tablesBenThu 6 AugData dictionaryNot started
Good tasks are small and checkable “Work on database” is not a task. “Build the Menu and Orders tables and enter five test records” is: you can tell whether it is finished. If a task takes longer than a few days, split it.

The Toolkit lists the eight items NESA says a process diary entry should include. Here is a template you can copy, then a weak and a strong entry for the same afternoon of work. The diary is also your best evidence that the work is yours (see Part 7), so make each entry specific enough that only the person who did the work could have written it.

Weak entry

Thursday. Worked on the project. Did the database. Had some problems but fixed them. Will do the screens next.

  • No name, no session detail, no task named.
  • “Some problems” hides the evidence of managing them.
  • No options, no reflection, no resources.

Strong entry

Thu 6 August, period 5 – Ben Tran. Since Tuesday I built the Menu and Orders tables and entered five test records (action plan tasks 4 and 5). Prices displayed as 5.5 instead of $5.50, so I changed the field format to $#.## from the data dictionary and re-entered the data. Next I have to choose between a drop-down and option buttons for picking items; I lean towards a drop-down because the manager has 40 items. If I had tested one record before entering five, I would have found the format problem sooner. Used: database software, Ana’s data dictionary, the manager’s menu list.

  • Every one of NESA’s eight items is present.
  • The problem, the fix and the result are all stated.
  • The reflection leads to a change in how the work is done.
Common mistakes Writing all the entries the night before the project is due (dates that do not match your version history give it away). Recording only successes. Copying a teammate’s entry. Leaving out the resources, including any AI tool you used.

Budgets

Researching and planning Costing time, hardware, software, training and ongoing use

A budget is a list of what the project will cost, added up, with a reserve for surprises. For a school project the main cost is time, and it is still worth pricing it, because a real client would be charged for it. Include the costs that come after the build: licences renew, cloud storage is billed monthly and people need training. A budget with only the build cost is a common weakness.

Template and worked example: project budget
ItemTypeBasisCost ($)

The figures are invented to show the layout. Use real quotes and rates for your own project, and state your assumptions.

In a spreadsheet, use SUM for the total and a formula such as =0.1*SUM(D2:D4) for the contingency so the total updates when a cost changes (see the spreadsheet functions in the Toolkit). A contingency is money or time set aside for things you cannot predict; choose an allowance, say why, and use your risk register to justify it.

Exam tip If a question asks how a budget helps manage a project, link it to decisions: it shows whether the project is affordable (feasibility), lets you compare options (for example build or outsource) and gives you a figure to check actual spending against.
Part 4 Working with a client and a team

Most project trouble is about people, not technology: a client who wanted something different, a team member who assumed someone else was doing a task, two versions of a file with the same name. NESA lists negotiating user and client needs and wants, and working collaboratively, among the criteria you should select for managing a project.

Ask yourself What is the last thing you asked someone to do and were sure they understood, but they did something else? What would have prevented it?

Gathering Requirements: Interview and Survey Templates

Applying tools Interviews and surveys to find out what the system must do and what limits it

A requirement is something the system must do or a limit it must respect. Interviews suit a small number of people whose answers you need in depth (the client, a key user). Surveys suit many people and simple answers. The syllabus lists both, with analytical reports, prototypes and presentations of research results (see the Enterprise Project page). Whichever you use, plan the questions in advance and keep a record.

Ethics and privacy If you interview or survey people, ask permission first, tell them why you are collecting the information and what you will do with it, keep responses secure and collect only what you need. Check your school’s rules before surveying other students. The Principles of Cybersecurity page covers privacy and personal information in more depth.

Negotiating Needs and Wants (MoSCoW)

Collaborating and managing criteria Sorting a long wish list into what the system must, should and could do

Clients usually want more than the time and money allow. NESA asks you to negotiate user and client needs and wants. A common technique for doing this is MoSCoW prioritisation. It is a widely used project-management method, not something NESA specifies, and the capital letters spell out the four categories (the lower-case o’s are just there to make a word).

CategoryMeaningTest
Must haveThe project fails without itIf we deliver everything else but not this, is the system useless?
Should haveImportant, but there is a workaround for nowWould the client be unhappy but still able to operate?
Could haveNice to have if time and money allowWould it improve things a little?
Won’t have (this time)Agreed as out of scope for nowWritten down, so it is not forgotten or promised by accident
Template and worked example: requirements by priority
RequirementWho askedPriorityReason
Students can order and pay before recessCanteen managerMustThe whole point of the system
Orders print in the kitchenCanteen managerMustStaff cannot fill orders without it
Allergy warnings on menu itemsParent surveyShouldSafety-related; a printed sign is a stop-gap
Loyalty pointsStudent surveyCouldPopular, but not needed to run the canteen
Mobile app for iPhone and AndroidStudent surveyWon’tOut of scope for the budget; a mobile-friendly web page is enough

How to negotiate

  1. Ask “what problem would this solve?” for each request. A want often has a simpler need behind it.
  2. Show the client the cost of each item in time and money, using your Gantt chart and budget.
  3. Offer options: “We can add loyalty points if we drop the print-out, or keep the print-out and add loyalty points next term.”
  4. Get the agreed list confirmed in writing (an email is enough) and record it in your diary.
  5. Treat changes after agreement as changes to the plan: what will move, and who agreed?

Uncontrolled additions during a project are known as scope creep. A signed-off priority list gives you a polite, factual way to say “yes, and this is what it will displace”.

Common mistakes Marking everything “Must”. Agreeing to a new feature in a hallway conversation without updating the plan. Confusing what the client asked for with what the client needs.

Collaboration, Version History and Roles

Working collaboratively Online collaboration tools, keeping track of versions and agreeing who does what

Online collaboration tools

Online collaboration is on NESA’s list of tools that support design and development. What matters is what the tool lets you do, not its brand:

NeedWhat to look forGood practice
Shared documents and filesCloud storage where several people can edit, with comments and suggestionsOne shared folder with an agreed structure and file-naming pattern
Version historyThe tool records who changed what and when, and lets you restore an earlier versionName major versions (for example booking-form_v0.3_2026-08-06) and note what changed

Cloud document platforms such as Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 keep a version history, and it can support your process diary: the timestamps show when work happened and who did it. Take regular backups anyway, and make sure your team has the right to use each shared file (a client’s data should go only where they have agreed).

Roles

NESA asks you to outline the role of the participants in a system, and a team needs roles too. Small teams share roles, but every task should still have one accountable person.

Template: team roles
RoleResponsible forName
Client (or client representative)Explaining the problem, answering questions, giving feedback and approving requirements and the final systemCanteen manager

In Scrum, one common agile framework, the client’s voice is a product owner who orders the backlog, and a scrum master helps the team follow the process. Those role names belong to Scrum, not to every project. If you work alone, you still play every role: say so, and note in your diary which hat you are wearing.

Exam tip When asked how collaboration improves a project, give a tool, a benefit and a risk: for example, shared documents with version history let the team work at the same time and recover mistakes, but only if everyone follows the same file-naming and permissions rules.
Part 5 Risk, feasibility and implementation

Before you commit weeks of work, ask two hard questions: what could go wrong, and can this really be delivered? After the build, ask a third: how will people actually start using it? NESA lists risk analysis, training, the preferred implementation method and testing the feasibility of the plan as parts of an implementation plan.

Ask yourself What is the single biggest risk to finishing your project on time, and what have you already done about it?

Risk Register

Risk analysis Listing what could go wrong, rating it and deciding what to do

A risk register is a table of things that might go wrong. It is a common project-management tool, not one that NESA specifies. For each risk you rate how likely it is and how serious the consequence would be, then decide on a response. Rating each on a 1 to 3 or 1 to 5 scale and multiplying them gives a score for ranking; the Cybersecurity page shows a full 5 by 5 risk matrix used in the same way. Whatever scale you choose, say what the numbers mean.

Template and worked example: risk register (scale 1 low to 3 high)
#RiskLikelihoodConsequenceScoreResponseOwner
1Client is unavailable for feedback for two weeks236Reduce: agree fixed meeting times now; keep a prototype ready to showProject manager
2Team member absent during the build224Reduce: share files and notes so another person can continueEveryone
3Database file is lost or corrupted133Reduce: automatic cloud backup plus a weekly copyDeveloper
4Requirements grow after sign-off (scope creep)326Avoid: signed MoSCoW list; changes go through the client meetingProject manager
5Testing finds a serious fault two days before handover236Reduce: test early and often; keep a contingency of timeTester

Choosing a response

  • Avoid the risk by changing the plan (drop a feature that depends on a tool nobody has used).
  • Reduce its likelihood or its consequence (backups, early testing, a second person who knows the task).
  • Transfer it to someone better placed to carry it (a supplier who guarantees their part).
  • Accept it and set aside time or money for it (your contingency), when the cost of a response is more than the risk.

Review the register whenever you update the Gantt chart. Add new risks as you find them, and mark risks that have passed. Note the ones that actually happened and how you handled them: that is diary material.

Exam tip Risk analysis is on NESA’s list for implementation plans. A strong answer names a specific risk from the scenario, rates it, and gives a response that is realistic for the organisation, rather than a general statement such as “back up your data”.

Feasibility Checklist

Testing the plan Checking that the project can be done before you commit to it

NESA asks you to “develop an implementation plan and test its feasibility”. A feasibility check asks whether a plan can be delivered. A common way to organise it is to look at four areas (some frameworks add a fifth, legal). This is a common framework, not a NESA list.

Template: feasibility checklist
AreaQuestionEvidenceYes, no or with changes
TechnicalDo we have, or can we get, the hardware, software and skills? Will it work with the systems already in use?Skills audit; tool trial; compatibility check
EconomicDo the benefits justify the costs, including ongoing costs?Budget; expected savings or benefits
ScheduleCan it be finished by the deadline with the people available?Gantt chart; critical path; risk register
OperationalWill the people who need it actually use it? Does it suit how they work?Interviews, prototype feedback, training plan

A “no” is not a failure: it is what the check is for. It tells you to change the plan, for example by reducing the scope, choosing a simpler tool, or asking for more time, before the work is done rather than after. Write down what you changed as a result and keep it in your diary.

Try this A netball club wants a website that takes online registrations and payments. Their volunteer has never built a website and there is no budget. Which of the four areas is the biggest concern, and how could the plan be changed?
Model answer

Technical and economic feasibility are the main concerns: the volunteer lacks the skills and payment handling usually has a cost and a security responsibility. The plan could be changed by using an existing registration service or a simple form instead of building payments, moving to end-user development with a tool the volunteer already knows, and putting online payment in the “won’t have this time” column.

Implementation Plan and Method

Developing an implementation plan How the new system goes live, and which changeover method to use

An implementation plan says how the new system will replace the old one (or be used for the first time): what happens, in what order, who does it and what if it goes wrong. NESA names four changeover methods, direct, parallel, pilot and phased. Their definitions and a scenario table are on the Toolkit page, and the Enterprise Project page covers the theory. Training and Documentation

Designing for ease of operation and maintenance Making sure people can use the system, and keep it running

A system nobody knows how to use has failed, however well it was built. NESA lists designing for ease of operation and maintenance, and asks you to trial the operation and maintenance documentation during testing. Plan training and documentation in stage 2 and produce them in stage 3, not in the last hour.

ItemAudienceWhat it contains
Quick reference cardEveryday usersThe five most common tasks, one screenshot each, in a page

Test your documentation Ask someone who has never seen the system to complete three tasks using only your user guide. Watch, do not help, and write down each place they get stuck. Fix the guide (or the system), then note what you changed. That is the “trialling the documentation” NESA describes.
Part 6 Testing and evaluation

Testing asks whether the system works. Evaluation asks whether it was worth doing and whether you managed the project well. They are different questions, and a project that only answers the first is only half finished.

Ask yourself If a stranger opened your finished project and typed nonsense into every box, what would happen?

Test Plan Template: Normal, Boundary and Erroneous Data

Methodology for testing the system Planning tests before you run them, with test data that covers the edges

Write the test plan before you test, and work out the expected result before you run each test. Otherwise you are only checking that the system does what it does. NESA names six testing methods (functional, acceptance, live data, simulated data, beta and volume), described on the Toolkit page. A common way to choose test data for each function is three groups:

  • Normal data: typical values the system should accept.
  • Boundary data: values at the edge of what is allowed, and just either side of it. Most faults hide here.
  • Erroneous data: values that should be rejected, such as text where a number is expected, a blank, a negative or an impossible date.
Template and worked example: test plan for an order quantity field (allowed: whole numbers 1 to 20)
TestFunctionTest dataTypeExpected resultActual resultPass or fail
1Enter quantity5NormalAccepted
2Enter quantity1Boundary (lowest allowed)Accepted
3Enter quantity20Boundary (highest allowed)Accepted
4Enter quantity0Boundary (just below)Rejected with a clear message
5Enter quantity21Boundary (just above)Rejected with a clear message
6Enter quantityabcErroneousRejected, no crash
7Enter quantity(left blank)ErroneousRejected, asks for a value
8Enter quantity2.5ErroneousRejected: whole numbers only

Running and recording the tests

  1. Fill in the expected column for every test before you run any of them.
  2. Run each test and record exactly what happened, including error messages. Take screenshots for the failures.
  3. Fix each fault, then re-run the failed test and the tests near it, because a fix can break something else. Record the retest.
  4. Finish with acceptance testing by the client against your success criteria, and a trial of the user guide.
Exam tip Question 18 of the 2025 HSC involved spreadsheet formulas checked with test data. When you are asked for test data, give a small set that includes normal, boundary and erroneous values and state the expected result for each. Just naming Just naming “testing” earns very little.ldquo;testingJust naming “testing” earns very little.rdquo; is too general.
Try this A form accepts a student’s year level, which must be a whole number from 7 to 12. Write six tests: two normal, two boundary and two erroneous, each with its expected result.
Model answer

Normal: 9 (accepted) and 11 (accepted). Boundary: 7 (accepted, lowest) and 12 (accepted, highest); you could add 6 and 13 (both rejected) as the values just outside. Erroneous: “nine” (rejected, text) and a blank (rejected, value required). 9.5 is another good erroneous value.

Verification and Validation in Practice

Verify and validate Did we build it correctly, and did we build the right thing?

The syllabus asks you to verify and validate an enterprise computing system. Applied to a whole project, the two words ask different questions. The theory is on the Enterprise Project page.

VerificationValidation
QuestionDid we build the system correctly?Did we build the right system?
Compared againstThe design and the requirements you wroteThe client’s real need and your success criteria
Typical activitiesFunctional tests, checking calculations and results against expected values, reviewing your diagrams against the buildAcceptance testing, a client trial, beta testing with real users, trialling the documentation
ExampleThe order total equals the sum of the item prices in every testThe canteen manager can actually run recess with it

A system can pass verification and fail validation: it meets the specification exactly, and the specification was wrong. That is why you go back to the client. You will also meet these words for data in the glossary: validation checks that a value is reasonable and allowed (for example a range check), and verification checks that it was entered or transferred correctly (for example double entry). Both ideas belong in a well-tested system.

Common mistakes Treating “it runs without errors” as proof that it is right. Testing only with data you invented and never with real records (with care for privacy). Letting the developer be the only tester.

Evaluating Against Criteria, and Maintenance

Evaluating and maintaining Judging the solution and the process against your own criteria, and planning its future

To evaluate is to make a judgement based on criteria (NESA’s glossary meaning). So your evaluation must go back to the success criteria you wrote in stage 1. Judge the product and the process: the product is the system; the process is how you managed the project.

Template: evaluation against success criteria

Evaluating the process

Compare your actual times with your planned times on the Gantt chart. Which tasks took longer, and why? Which risks happened? What did the client feedback change? What would you do differently with the same project again? Your process diary already holds this evidence: quote it. An evaluation that only says “it went well” is a claim; one that names a task, a number and a decision is evidence.

Maintenance

Systems need looking after after they go live. Plan for it. Maintenance commonly includes fixing faults found in use, adapting the system when something around it changes (a new device, a new rule, a new supplier) and improving it in response to new requests. In your notes, say who is responsible, how a fault is reported, how often data is backed up and how updates will be tested before they go live. The requests you placed in the “could” and “won’t” rows of your MoSCoW table are the start of the future improvements list. NESA also lists modifying designs to improve functionality: record what you would change in the design and why.

Exam tip “Evaluate” means to make a judgement based on criteria. Name the criteria, give evidence for each, then state your overall judgement. Listing what the system does is description, not evaluation.
Part 7 Assessment and academic integrity

How project work is assessed is decided largely by your school, within rules set by NESA. Those rules changed recently, so this part sets out what NESA has published and what it has not. It also explains what “your own work” means when generative AI tools are everywhere.

Ask yourself If your teacher asked you to explain how you built a part of your project, could you? What evidence would you point to?

How Enterprise Computing Is Assessed

Facts from NESA School-based assessment, the take-home task limit and the HSC examination

Follow your school’s assessment schedule This section summarises NESA pages checked on 28 September 2026 and is a study aid only. Your school gives you a formal assessment schedule at the start of the course, and written notice before each task. Rules can change: always follow your school’s schedule and task notifications, and ask your teacher if anything is unclear.

School-based assessment

  • Components and weightings. For both Year 11 and Year 12, NESA sets two mandatory course components, each worth 50%: knowledge and understanding of course content, and knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content.
  • Number and type of tasks. Schools decide the number, type and weighting of assessment tasks, within the limit below. NESA’s guidance (not a rule) is to use a variety of tasks and to weight each task between 10% and 40%.
  • Project work. On the pages we checked, NESA does not publish a mandatory Enterprise Project task, a project weighting, a length or a word limit. Whether and how your project work is assessed is your school’s decision, so ask your teacher.
  • Notice. Under NESA’s assessment rules, schools give students written notice of a task, typically 14 calendar days, with its weighting, the outcomes assessed and the marking criteria where appropriate.

The take-home task limit

NESA has announced that school-based assessment programs can have no more than one take-home task, worth no more than 15%. A take-home task is one completed outside a classroom, a formal examination or teacher-approved monitoring conditions. The limit starts in Term 4 2026 for HSC courses and Term 1 2027 for Year 11 (Preliminary) courses. It does not apply to courses with a major project or an HSC practical exam. Enterprise Computing has neither (the HSC is a written online examination), and it is not on the list of exempt courses NESA published, so as we read that notice the limit applies to it. Your school can tell you how it affects your tasks. Schools still decide the number, type and weighting of tasks completed in class or under supervision.

The HSC examination

The HSC examination is a single written paper done on a computer: 80 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes including 10 minutes reading time, with objective-response items (about 20 marks) and short-answer items (about 60 marks). There is no separate practical examination or submitted major work. Project skills still appear in the exam: the 2025 marker feedback included questions on project management tools (Question 22) and on offshore development and freelance work (Question 24).

Academic Integrity and Generative AI

Your own work What NESA requires of schools and how to keep evidence that the work is yours

NESA’s ACE rules require schools to design formal assessment tasks so that marks reflect a student’s own knowledge, skills and understanding, and not the use of generative artificial intelligence. NESA has also said that schools should not rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice. The emphasis is on how schools design tasks and gather evidence of a student’s own work.

In practice that puts the responsibility on you to be able to show how your work was made. Your school will explain what is allowed for each task. Ask before you use any AI tool for any part of a task, and follow the answer.

Keep evidence of your own work

  • A process diary with regular, dated, specific entries (see the template and examples). It records your decisions, problems and reflections, which are hard to fake later.
  • Version history in your files, showing the project growing in steps over real dates.
  • Drafts, sketches and earlier versions, including the ones you threw away.
  • Client and teacher feedback and what you changed because of it.
  • Sources for anything you did not create yourself: images, code, data, text. Note the licence or permission (see the Creative Commons entry in the glossary).
  • The ability to explain it. If you can walk a teacher through any part of your project and say why you did it that way, you have the strongest evidence of all.
A simple habit If you use any tool that generates or rewrites text, code, images or data, and your school permits it, record in your diary which tool, what you asked, what it gave you and what you changed or rejected. Keeping that record is honest, and it shows your own judgement.
What not to do Do not submit work you cannot explain, work made by someone else (or by a tool) without acknowledgement where your school requires it, or a diary written after the event. Copying another team member’s entries is not collaboration.
