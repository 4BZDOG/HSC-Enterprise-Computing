Why this matters now

Every scroll, tap and swipe on your phone is interactive media: a team decided where the button sits, how big the text is, what happens when you press it and what the app learns from you. Those decisions decide whether you finish a task, feel respected or quietly give away more data than you meant to.

In this topic you will investigate how interactive media communicates, judge its social, ethical and legal issues (including Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property), choose file formats and compression, design a user interface with real design tools, and build and publish your own front-end and data journalism projects.

Try this as you learn Pick one app you use daily and list three design decisions that keep you using it. Which of them serve you, and which serve the company?

Syllabus Part 1 Ubiquity of interactive media

Interactive media is everywhere, from the streaming service you watch to the game you play with friends, and each example makes choices about your data, your attention and your access. This part looks at how it communicates, its legal and ethical issues, where it came from and how it shapes behaviour, marketing and identity.

Communicating with Interactive Media

🎯 (EC-11-01, EC-11-11)

📌 Investigate how interactive media and the user experience (UX) is used to communicate information to an audience

Interactive media is a two-way conversation

NESA defines interactive media as the use of digital technologies to present combinations of text, graphics, video, animation and/or sound in an integrated way, where the user can interact with the result. Games, media-rich websites and e-books are all examples. A poster or a radio broadcast sends a message one way. Interactive media sends a message and then listens: every tap, search, pause, share and abandoned form tells the sender something about whether the message worked.

To investigate how it communicates, break any example into five parts: the sender and its purpose, the message and the media used to encode it, the channel and device, the audience, and the feedback that returns to the sender. Anything that gets in the way of the message between sender and audience (slow loading, tiny text, no captions, confusing navigation) is a barrier. Removing barriers is the job of user experience design.

Matching the medium to the audience and purpose

No single medium suits every message. Effective communication starts by asking who the audience is (age, language, prior knowledge, devices, abilities, connection speed) and what they should know or do afterwards.

What user experience (UX) adds

NESA's glossary defines user experience (UX) as "the usability, ease of use, and enjoyment provided in the interaction between the customer and the product". Good UX means people can reach their goal, with little effort and few errors, and feel positive about it. International standard ISO 9241-210 describes this way of working as human-centred design: involve real users early, understand the context of use, and iterate from evidence. UX is broader than how a screen looks. It includes speed, clarity of wording, error messages, accessibility and support.

Usability heuristics as investigation criteria

To investigate an interface systematically, use published criteria rather than personal taste. The best known are the ten usability heuristics developed by usability researcher Jakob Nielsen in the 1990s and published by the Nielsen Norman Group. A heuristic evaluation has one or more reviewers work through an interface and record which heuristics it breaks, where, and how serious the problem is.

Scenario A regional council's event page loads slowly on phones, hides the date in an image with no alternative text, and gives no confirmation after "Register". Against the heuristics: visibility of system status (no confirmation), error prevention and recovery (no message when the form fails), and, for accessibility, a missing text alternative. Fixes are cheap: real text for the date, a clear confirmation message and compressed images.
HSC exam focus "Investigate" asks you to look closely at evidence and reach a supported conclusion. Name the audience and purpose, then judge the interface against named criteria such as visibility of system status, consistency or accessibility. A full-mark answer links each judgement to something specific you can see in the example, and says how it changes what the audience understands or does.

Social, Ethical and Legal Issues

🎯 (EC-11-07)

📌 Investigate social, ethical and legal issues when developing and implementing interactive media systems

Three lenses on the same decision

Every interactive media project raises questions that are legal (what the law requires), ethical (what is right, even if it is legal) and social (how the system affects people and communities). The lenses often disagree. A tracking script may be legal and still be ethically doubtful if users cannot understand it. Investigating means examining each issue from the point of view of the people affected, not only the developer.

Issues arise at different stages

When developing a system the questions are about inputs: Do we have the right to use these images, fonts, music and data? Have the people in our photos and videos agreed, and are they old enough to agree? Are we testing with people who use assistive technology? When implementing (releasing and running) the system the questions are about ongoing effects: What data do we keep, for how long and who can see it? How will we respond to a breach, a complaint or a takedown request? Who moderates user comments?

Scenario A NSW public high school builds a showcase site for Year 11 multimedia projects. Before launch the team checks: consent (written permission for every student's name and photo, with a way to withdraw); copyright (every song, image and font is original, licensed or Creative Commons with attribution); ICIP (any use of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander art, language or stories has been discussed with the local community and the school's Aboriginal education staff, with permission recorded); accessibility (captions, alt text and keyboard navigation); and security (comments moderated; no personal details in file names). None of this is optional decoration. Each item prevents a specific harm.

Distinguish "the law says" from "it would be more ethical to". When asked about development and implementation, show at least one issue from each stage.

Evolution of Interactive Media

🎯 (EC-11-06, EC-11-07)

📌 Research the evolution of interactive media

  • prevalence of blogs, online video and digital radio
  • privacy issues and the use of intellectual property, including Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP)

A short history of interactive media

Interactive media has changed because three things changed together: bandwidth (from dial-up to broadband and mobile data), devices (from desktop computers to smartphones, tablets and headsets) and tools (from hand-coded pages to platforms that let anyone publish). Each shift lowered the cost of creating and sharing media and raised the number of people who can take part.

PhaseWhat changedTypical interactive media
Early web (1990s)Pages published by specialists; slow connections; mostly text and small imagesStatic websites, email, early online games, CD-ROMs
Participatory web (2000s)Publishing tools for everyone; users create the contentBlogs, wikis, photo sharing, video sites, podcasts
Mobile and social (from about 2007)Smartphones, app stores, always-on connections, social feedsApps, short video, live streaming, location-aware services
Streaming and immersive (2010s onward)Fast broadband and 4G/5G; cloud services; headsets and AR on phonesOn-demand video and audio, multiplayer online games, AR and VR experiences

Prevalence of blogs, online video and digital radio

Blogs (from "weblog") began as personal online journals in the late 1990s. Easy platforms such as Blogger (launched 1999) and WordPress (2003) let people publish without writing HTML. Blogs turned readers into publishers, gave niche communities and independent journalists a voice, and led to the comment sections, feeds and subscriptions that later social media adopted. Many organisations now run blogs to communicate news and build trust.

Online video became mainstream once broadband and better compression (see digitising assets) made streaming practical. YouTube, founded in 2005, made it easy for anyone to upload and share video. Streaming services, live streaming and short-form video followed. Video is now a main way audiences learn, shop and are entertained, which is why captions, file formats and performance matter.

Digital radio moves audio from analogue broadcast to digital delivery. In Australia, DAB+ digital radio began broadcasting in the capital cities in 2009, offering more stations and extra data such as station and song details. Radio also moved online through internet streaming and podcasts: audio files delivered by subscription that people play when and where they choose. This on-demand model changed radio from a schedule to a library.

Research tip When you research, record the source and date for every fact, prefer primary or official sources (a company's own announcement, a regulator, a national broadcaster), and cross-check dates in two places. Treat statistics about audience size as "reported by" a named source, because figures vary and go out of date.

Privacy issues and the use of intellectual property

As media became participatory, two problems grew: personal information became valuable, and it became easy to copy other people's work.

Privacy issues

Early websites collected little. Modern interactive media collects data trails: accounts, cookies, device and location data, purchases and behaviour. That data enables personalisation but also profiling, targeted advertising and identity theft after a breach. In Australia the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) set rules for how covered organisations collect, use, store and disclose personal information. Under the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme they must notify affected people and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) about eligible breaches likely to cause serious harm. The 2022 breaches at Optus and Medibank, in which customers' personal information was accessed by criminals, made many Australians more aware of how much data organisations hold and how long they keep it. Since then, changes such as the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024, which created a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, have increased the pressure on organisations.

Intellectual property (IP)

Copyright is a form of intellectual property. Under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth), copyright arises automatically when an original work (text, image, music, film, software) is created and recorded. There is no registration. The owner, usually the creator unless an employment contract or assignment says otherwise, controls copying, publishing and adapting the work. Copyright lasts for a long time (for most works, the life of the creator plus 70 years), so most media found online is not free to reuse. Creators also have moral rights: to be attributed, not to be falsely credited and not to have the work treated in a derogatory way.

Australia does not have a general "fair use" rule. It has specific fair dealing exceptions, such as research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, and reporting news, each with limits. "It's for a school project" does not automatically make copying legal. Instead, choose media with a clear licence: your own work, work you have permission to use, public-domain work, or work under an open licence such as Creative Commons (see supporting creative processes). The Copyright Act 1968 is on the Federal Register of Legislation, and the Australian Copyright Council explains it in plain language at copyright.org.au.

Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP)

Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) is the term used for the rights that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples hold in their cultural heritage: for example their knowledge, stories, languages, art, designs, music, dance, ceremonies and cultural sites. ICIP belongs to communities and is connected to Country, kinship and cultural responsibility. It is not simply a resource for other people to use.

Copyright law fits ICIP poorly. Copyright generally protects an individual creator's original work for a limited time. ICIP is often communal, passed down through generations, and continues to live in the community. As Dr Terri Janke, an Indigenous Australian solicitor who has led this work for many years, and others have explained, Australian copyright law does not on its own prevent the misuse, distortion or alteration of communally owned cultural material. That is why ethical protocols matter.

The Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property in the Arts, authored by Dr Terri Janke and published through the Australia Council for the Arts (now Creative Australia), guide artists, designers and media makers. Its principles include respect for cultural heritage, self-determination, communication, consultation and consent, correct interpretation, cultural integrity, protection of secret or sacred material, attribution, benefit sharing and the recognition and protection of cultures. (Check the current edition for the exact wording.) Terri Janke and Company's True Tracks framework applies similar ideas to business and organisations.

Practical rules for your projects Do not copy Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander art, designs, stories, language or music from the internet into your media. Ask who the custodians are, contact them (through the school's Aboriginal education staff, a local Aboriginal Education Consultative Group or an Aboriginal community organisation) and get permission for the specific use. Credit the community and the artist. Share any benefit fairly. Some communities have protocols about showing the names or images of people who have passed away, so check the guidance that comes with the material.

Podcast

When ICIP appears, say clearly that the issue is consultation and consent with the relevant community, not only "giving credit".

Interactive Media in Enterprises

🎯 (EC-11-01)

📌 Describe the contribution of interactive media systems to a range of enterprises

  • how simulation, gamification and augmented reality (AR) support online training and learning
  • how streaming services, gaming and virtual reality (VR) improve access to entertainment
  • how specialist apps support delivery tracking, advertising and communication

An enterprise is an activity that produces goods or services, from a corner shop to a national broadcaster. Interactive media contributes to enterprises by reaching customers, training staff, delivering services and gathering data. The three groups below correspond to the three NESA "including" points.

Simulation, gamification and AR in online training and learning

A simulation is a digital model that lets people practise a task and see the results without real-world risk or cost. Flight, driving and machinery simulators, and clinical simulations that let nursing students practise patient assessment, are common examples. Learners can repeat difficult or dangerous tasks as often as they need, and the system can record every decision for feedback.

Gamification is, in NESA's words, the adjustment of a task using digital technologies into a computer game in an attempt to make it more appealing. Points, badges, levels, streaks and leaderboards add motivation to routine training such as safety modules or language practice. It works best when the game elements reward genuine learning; it can backfire if learners chase points and ignore content.

Augmented reality (AR) overlays computer-generated information on the real environment, through a phone camera or smart glasses. In training, AR can label the parts of a machine as a trainee looks at it or show a repair step in place, so that instructions appear where they are needed. Because AR relies on a camera, location and sensors, it also raises privacy questions about what is being captured.

Streaming, gaming and VR improving access to entertainment

Streaming services deliver video and audio over the internet on demand, so viewers watch when and where they like without buying a disc or waiting for a broadcast. Australian audiences use national broadcasters' catch-up services and commercial and international subscription services. Streaming reduces the barriers of distance, timing and cost: a viewer in a remote town can watch the same programs as a viewer in the city, provided they have a reliable connection. Video games, from phone games to console and PC titles, bring interactive stories and shared play to people of all ages, and online play lets friends in different places play together.

Virtual reality (VR) places the user inside a simulated three-dimensional environment using a headset and motion tracking. It can give access to experiences that are otherwise out of reach, such as touring a museum, sitting in a concert audience or exploring a place without travelling, and it can be a comfortable way to take part for people with limited mobility. Its limits are cost, the need for space and powerful hardware, and motion discomfort for some users.

Scenario A regional hospital trains nursing students with a clinical simulation and gives them a points-and-badges quiz for medication safety (gamification). A logistics firm's app shows a parcel's status and estimated arrival on a map, and messages the customer if it is delayed. A farm co-op's app lets members receive market price alerts and message the co-op's buyers. In each case the enterprise gains reach and speed, and the users need clear privacy information and a way to opt out of alerts.

Hardware Performance Requirements

🎯 (EC-11-08, EC-11-10)

📌 Evaluate the performance requirements of hardware for specific interactive media projects

Evaluating means judging against criteria

To evaluate hardware for an interactive media project, first list what the project must do, then compare hardware against those requirements, then make a justified judgement. Working out storage and bandwidth

Calculating file sizes turns "large" into evidence. The examples below show why compression is essential.

  • One uncompressed 1080p frame: 1920 × 1080 pixels × 3 bytes (24-bit colour) = 6,220,800 bytes, about 6.2 MB.
  • One second at 30 frames per second: 6,220,800 × 30 = 186,624,000 bytes, about 187 MB. One minute is about 11.2 GB. Editing and streaming video are only practical because of compression.
  • CD-quality stereo audio: 44,100 samples/s × 16 bits × 2 channels = 1,411,200 bits per second, about 10.6 MB per minute.

Making the judgement

Scenario A school's media club will edit short documentaries in full HD. Option A is a standard laptop with 8 GB RAM, integrated graphics and a 256 GB drive. Option B is a laptop with 32 GB RAM, a dedicated GPU and a 1 TB SSD. Against the requirements: Option A can edit, but timelines will stutter, exports will be slow and 256 GB fills after a few projects, so storage would need to be added. Option B meets the need with room to grow but costs more and is heavier. Judgement: Option B, or A with extra fast external storage if the budget is tight, because the club's requirement is smooth editing of large files; RAM and storage matter more than display size. Note the trade-off explicitly and state the assumptions (these specifications are examples only).
HSC exam focus "Evaluate" needs criteria, evidence and a judgement. State the project's requirements first, compare at least two options against them, weigh cost, performance, portability and the audience's devices, and finish with a clear recommendation with a reason. Listing components without a decision earns few marks.

Supporting Creative Processes

🎯 (EC-11-06, EC-11-07)

📌 Explain how interactive media systems can support creative processes

  • sandbox gaming
  • social media
  • digital creative commons

A creative process moves from an idea to a finished work through cycles of making, sharing, feedback and improving. Interactive media systems support that process by lowering the cost of trying ideas, letting creators show work-in-progress to an audience, and letting others build on it. The three NESA examples show three different supports.

Sandbox gaming

NESA describes sandbox gaming as a virtual world where players can build their playscapes, invite other players to play and test game play. Games such as Minecraft and platforms such as Roblox give players building blocks, scripting tools and a shared world rather than a fixed storyline. They support creative processes in these ways:

  • Ideate and prototype: players sketch a build or a mini-game quickly and change it instantly.
  • Test with an audience: friends play the creation and the creator sees where they get stuck, which is usability testing in miniature.
  • Learn design and coding: logic blocks, redstone circuits or a scripting language teach systems thinking. Education editions of these games are used in classrooms for design and history projects.
  • Collaborate: several players build one world together, dividing roles as a real production team would.

The issues to weigh are moderation of user-made content, protection of young players in shared spaces, and who owns what players create. That last point is set by each platform's terms of use.

Social media

Social media supports creative work at every stage: finding inspiration and references, sharing drafts for feedback, collaborating across distance, building a portfolio and reaching an audience. A student filmmaker can post a trailer, read the comments and re-cut the ending. Remix features (duets, stitching, sharing a template) let creators build on each other's work, which raises questions of permission and credit. Algorithms decide what is seen, so reach is not a fair measure of quality.

Digital creative commons

Creative Commons (CC) is a non-profit organisation that enables the sharing of creative content without the need to seek legal approval each time (NESA glossary). Creators choose a standard licence that tells everyone in advance what they may do. Because permission is built in, others can legally reuse, adapt and combine work, which is a creative process in its own right.

Licence elementSymbolWhat it means for a reuser
AttributionBYYou must credit the creator. Every CC licence except CC0 requires this.
ShareAlikeSAIf you adapt the work, you must share your new work under the same licence.
NonCommercialNCYou may not use the work mainly for commercial advantage or payment.

The elements combine into six licences, from the most open (CC BY) to the most restrictive (CC BY-NC-ND). Good practice is TASL attribution: Title, Author, Source, Licence. For example (a made-up credit): "Reading Room" by Jordan Lee, from example.org/reading-room, licensed CC BY 4.0. Check that the person offering the work actually owns it, and check the licence version and its link. A CC licence does not remove other rights such as privacy or ICIP. A CC licence on a photo of a person does not give you permission to use their image in an advertisement, and material based on Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander cultural heritage may need community consent even when it appears online with a CC label.

Scenario A Year 11 group is making an interactive tour of their town. They build the base map in a sandbox game, ask followers on social media for old photos, and use only CC BY or CC0 images from a public collection, crediting each with TASL. They share the finished tour under CC BY-NC so other classes can reuse it for free but not sell it.
HSC exam focus "Explain" asks how and why. For each example give the mechanism (for instance, sandbox play lets creators test ideas on real players) and the effect on the creative process. When Creative Commons appears, name at least two licence elements and what each allows or restricts.

Influencing Human Behaviour

🎯 (EC-11-01, EC-11-07)

📌 Examine how human behaviour may be influenced by interactive media, including opportunities for people with disability to explore and participate in their environment

How interactive media influences behaviour

Designers use knowledge of how people think and feel to shape what they do. This is called persuasive design when it helps people reach their own goals and manipulative design when it works against them. Most techniques can be used either way. What separates them is transparency and whose interests they serve.

Digital Marketing and Consumer Behaviour

🎯 (EC-11-04, EC-11-07)

📌 Investigate how digital marketing techniques influence consumer behaviour

  • guided choice (nudging)
  • default settings, including cookies
  • autofill
  • pop-ups promoting online shopping

Digital marketing promotes products and services through online channels such as search, social media, email, apps and websites. Because every click is measurable, marketers can test small design changes and keep the ones that increase sales. Understanding these techniques lets you use them responsibly and recognise when you are being steered.

Guided choice (nudging)

A nudge is a design choice that steers people towards an option without forbidding any other. The idea comes from behavioural economics and was popularised by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein in their book Nudge (2008). The way options are arranged is the choice architecture. Examples in online shopping include:

  • a "Recommended" or "Most popular" badge on one product (social proof);
  • listing a mid-priced plan between a very cheap and a very expensive one so that it looks reasonable;
  • a "Frequently bought together" bundle;
  • a pre-selected delivery option.

A nudge can serve the user (a default that protects privacy, a healthy option placed first) or the seller only (an expensive add-on ticked by default). A fair test: would the user be comfortable if they understood exactly what the nudge was doing?

Default settings, including cookies

Most people never change a default, a tendency called the default effect (or status quo bias). Whoever chooses the default therefore makes the decision for most users. Defaults appear in settings such as location sharing, marketing emails, auto-renewing subscriptions, and pre-ticked boxes for optional extras.

Cookies are small pieces of data a website stores in your browser. Session cookies last until you close the browser and keep you logged in or hold your cart. Persistent cookies last longer and remember preferences. Third-party cookies, set by advertising or analytics companies embedded in the page, can follow you across many sites and build a profile. Essential cookies are needed for the site to work; analytics and advertising cookies are optional. Cookie banners exist because some regions, notably the European Union, require consent before setting non-essential cookies. Australia has no single "cookie law", but the Privacy Act 1988 and the APPs apply when cookies and similar identifiers relate to an identifiable person, so notice and transparency still matter. The default in the banner matters: an unticked box and an equal-weight "Reject" button respect the user, while pre-ticked boxes and a hidden "Manage options" link steer them.

    Autofill

    Autofill lets a browser or password manager fill in saved names, addresses, card details and passwords. It reduces typing and errors, and is a real accessibility gain for people who find typing difficult. It is also a marketing tool, because every removed step makes it easier to complete a purchase. The risks: a shared device can expose saved details, and a malicious form can include hidden fields that quietly collect autofilled data. Good practice is to use a reputable password manager, keep card autofill for trusted sites, use a device lock, and turn autofill off on shared computers. Designers should mark fields with the correct autocomplete attributes so autofill works, and ask only for what is needed.

    Pop-ups promoting online shopping

    Pop-ups appear over a page to grab attention. Common shopping examples are a "10% off if you subscribe" window, an exit-intent pop-up when the cursor heads for the tab bar, a reminder of items left in the cart, a countdown timer, and "Sam from Parramatta just bought this" notices. They work because they interrupt at a decision point. They also annoy users, cover content, and can trap keyboard and screen-reader users if they are not built correctly. Sending marketing email or SMS to people who sign up needs their consent and a working unsubscribe under the Spam Act 2003.

    Dark patterns and the law

    Design that steers people into choices that are not in their interests is called a dark pattern (a term coined by user experience designer Harry Brignull), also called deceptive design. Examples include confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't like saving money"), extra fees revealed only at the last step, a subscription that is easy to start and hard to cancel, and fake scarcity or fake countdowns.

    In Australia, the Australian Consumer Law prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce and false representations, and the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) enforces it. In 2022 the Federal Court ordered Google to pay $60 million in penalties after the ACCC showed that Google had misled some Android users about what location data it collected. Google said one setting controlled this when another also enabled collection (see the ACCC media release). The case shows that how a settings screen is worded can be a legal matter.

    Social Media and Human Connection

    🎯 (EC-11-01, EC-11-07)

    📌 Evaluate social media applications that encourage human connections

    • crowdsourcing through a social media app
    • learning via massive online open courses (MOOCs)
    • gaming platforms that encourage massive multiplayer online games (MMOGs)

    Criteria for evaluating a connection platform

    Evaluating means judging value against criteria. For a platform that is meant to connect people, useful criteria are:

    • Quality of connection: does it help people form real relationships, share knowledge or work together?
    • Inclusion and accessibility: who can join (cost, age limits, language, disability)?
    • Safety and moderation: can users report abuse, and does the platform act?
    • Privacy and data use: what does it collect, and can users control it?
    • Wellbeing and design: does it help users stop, or keep them hooked?
    • Reliability and reach: does it work well on ordinary devices and connections?

    Crowdsourcing through a social media app

    Crowdsourcing uses a large group of people to contribute information, ideas or work, usually online. Social media makes it fast: a community page asks residents to report potholes, a fan group identifies a song, and during a flood or bushfire people share observations and photos. Citizen-science apps, where users upload photos of plants and animals for experts to identify, collect far more sightings than a few researchers could.

    Strengths: speed, scale, local knowledge and low cost. Weaknesses: accuracy and bias (who takes part, who does not), rumours spreading as fact, privacy of people in photos, and no accountability. Organisations reduce these risks by verifying reports, using moderators and showing sources.

    Learning through massive online open courses (MOOCs)

    A massive online open course (MOOC) is an online course open to a very large number of learners, often free to start. Coursera, edX, FutureLearn and Open Universities Australia host courses from universities and other providers. Learners use videos, readings, quizzes and discussion forums, and peers connect in those forums.

    Strengths: access regardless of location, flexible pace, low or no cost, and courses in specialist topics. Weaknesses: many learners do not finish, there is little individual support, a reliable internet connection is needed, discussion can be uneven, and employers and institutions vary in how much they recognise a certificate.

    Gaming platforms and massive multiplayer online games (MMOGs)

    A massive multiplayer online game (MMOG) lets very large numbers of players share one persistent online world. Players form teams and guilds, trade, chat by voice and text, and take on shared goals. For many players, especially those who are isolated or live in remote areas, these communities offer friendships and a sense of belonging. Skills such as teamwork, leadership and problem solving develop through play.

    The risks are contact with strangers, bullying and harassment, exposure to inappropriate content, in-game spending (including "loot box" mechanics that resemble gambling), and time displacing sleep and study. Platforms respond with parental controls, chat filters, reporting tools and spending limits. The eSafety Commissioner publishes guidance for young people and families.

    Digital Identities and Profiling

    🎯 (EC-11-03, EC-11-07)

    📌 Explore how interactive media platforms support the creation of digital identities

    • personal e-profiles
    • identifier versus identity
    • profiling and auto-profiling
    • privacy settings

    Personal e-profiles

    A personal e-profile is the account page you build on a platform: display name, photo, biography, links, interests, and sometimes date of birth, school or workplace. You choose these details, so a profile is a deliberate presentation of yourself. People use different profiles for different audiences: a professional one, a gaming one, a school one. Interactive media platforms support profile creation with templates, prompts, verified badges, follower counts and portfolios. What you put in a profile is durable and searchable, and can be copied or screenshotted, so consider what a future employer or university might see.

    Identifier versus identity

    An identifier is a value that points to one account, person or device: a username, email address, phone number, IP address, device ID or cookie ID (and in the offline world, a student number or a Medicare number). An identity is richer: the collection of characteristics, behaviours, relationships and reputation that makes up who you are online and how others and systems see you.

    Your digital footprint is the data you leave behind (NESA glossary). The active part is what you post and submit. The passive part is collected without you doing anything: your IP address, device details and browsing history. Identifiers connect these traces so they add up to a profile, even if you never give your name. Government "digital ID" systems, such as myID used with Australian Government services, are a different use of the words: a way to prove who you are to a service under a legal framework, the Digital ID Act 2024.

    Profiling and auto-profiling

    Profiling is building a description of a person from data, in order to predict what they want, like or will do. It can be done by people (a manager checking your public posts) or systems. Auto-profiling means the system does it automatically, by inferring characteristics from your behaviour without you entering them: your likely age range, interests, income or life events, from what you watch, buy, search and where you go.

    Profiles power the "For you" page, product recommendations, personalised prices and offers, and the ads you see. They are useful, but they can be wrong, can lock in stereotypes and bias, can feel intrusive, and can be exposed in a breach. Individuals can ask organisations covered by the Privacy Act what personal information they hold about them, and can generally ask for it to be corrected. The OAIC explains those rights and the APPs on its website (see Australian Privacy Principles).

    Privacy settings

    Privacy settings are the controls that let you decide what data is collected, who can see your profile and content, and how your information is used. Defaults are often more open than you might choose, so check them rather than assume. A short audit:

    1. Set the profile and posts to private or friends-only where possible.
    2. Turn off location sharing and remove location from photos you share.
    3. Limit who can tag, message or find you by phone number or email.
    4. Review ad personalisation and turn off cross-site tracking if offered.
    5. Check which apps and games have access to your account and remove those you no longer use.
    6. Use unique passwords, a password manager and multi-factor authentication.
    7. Repeat every few months, because platforms change their settings.
    Scenario Jordan, 16, joins a new video app with the school email address. The app asks for a name and photo (an e-profile), sets the account to public by default, and starts recommending videos based on watch time (auto-profiling). Jordan switches the account to private, disables contact syncing, turns off personalised ads and reviews the settings again next term. Nothing about the app changed, but Jordan's identity is now more under Jordan's control.

    Digitising images: resolution and colour depth

    A bitmap image is a grid of pixels. Resolution is how many pixels there are (for example 1920 × 1080), or for scanning and print, how many dots per inch (dpi). More pixels capture more detail and make larger files. Colour depth (bit depth per pixel) is how many bits describe each pixel's colour: 1 bit gives 2 colours (black and white), 8 bits give 256 colours, and 24 bits (8 each for red, green and blue) give 224 = 16,777,216 colours.

    Uncompressed size = width × height × colour depth ÷ 8 (bytes). Worked example: an A4 page (8.27 × 11.69 inches) scanned at 300 dpi is about 2481 × 3507 pixels = 8,700,867 pixels. At 24-bit colour that is 8,700,867 × 3 bytes = 26,102,601 bytes, about 26 MB before compression. Scanning at 600 dpi would multiply the pixel count by four. Use the resolution you need: about 300 dpi is common for print; a screen usually needs far fewer pixels than a print scan.

    Digitising sound: sampling rate and bit depth

    Sound is a continuous wave. Digitising samples it: measuring the wave's height many times per second. The sampling rate (in hertz) is how many measurements per second; the bit depth is how many bits record each measurement, which sets how many different heights can be stored. A higher sampling rate captures faster changes; a higher bit depth captures finer differences in loudness. To capture a frequency correctly you need to sample at more than twice that frequency (the Nyquist rule). People hear up to about 20 kHz, so audio CDs use 44,100 samples per second (44.1 kHz) with 16 bits (65,536 possible heights) per sample per channel. Video production commonly uses 48 kHz.

    Worked example: a 3-minute (180 s) stereo recording at 44,100 Hz and 16 bits: 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bits per second; × 180 seconds = 254,016,000 bits, which is 31,752,000 bytes, about 31.8 MB uncompressed. An MP3 of the same track is a small fraction of that size.

    Digitising video

    Video is a sequence of images (frames) plus sound. Its size depends on resolution, frame rate (frames per second), colour depth and length, and then on the bitrate chosen by the codec. As calculated earlier, uncompressed high-definition video is enormous, so video is always compressed for storage and streaming.

    Data compression

    NESA's glossary defines data compression as a reduction in the number of bits needed to represent data. It saves storage and speeds up transmission. There are two families.

    Lossless compression

    Lossless compression finds and removes redundancy (repeated patterns) but keeps every bit of information, so the original can be perfectly rebuilt. A simple technique is run-length encoding: WWWWWWBBBW can be stored as 6W3B1W. Real formats use more advanced methods. PNG, FLAC and ZIP are lossless. What is discarded: nothing. Artefacts: none. Trade-off: the saving is limited, especially for detailed photographs and natural sound. Use it when exactness matters: text, code, logos and screenshots, master copies, and archives.

    Lossy compression

    Lossy compression permanently discards information that people are unlikely to notice. The discarded data cannot be recovered. It can shrink files a great deal, which is why photographs, music and video are delivered this way.

    • JPEG splits an image into small blocks, then reduces fine detail and colour resolution that the eye is less sensitive to. Too much compression produces artefacts: blocky squares, blurred edges and ringing around sharp lines such as text.
    • MP3 and AAC remove sounds masked by louder ones and frequencies most people cannot hear. At low bitrates you may hear a thin, watery sound or smeared sharp sounds.
    • H.264 video stores full frames only occasionally and then just the changes between frames. At low bitrates you see blocks in fast motion and smearing or banding in smooth gradients.

    Every re-save of a lossy file discards more, called generation loss. Edit from a lossless master and export a lossy copy once, at the end. Use lossy compression when the audience cannot notice the loss and file size or speed matters most: web photos, streaming audio and video, and mobile delivery.

    How the UI Shapes the UX

    🎯 (EC-11-08, EC-11-10)

    📌 Explain how the user interface (UI) impacts on the user experience (UX)

    The relationship

    NESA's glossary defines the user interface (UI) as the means by which users interact with computer hardware or software. In software this is fields for text and number entry, mouse pointers, buttons and other graphical elements; in hardware it is switches, dials and lights. The user experience (UX) is the whole result of that interaction: usability, ease of use and enjoyment. The UI is what the user meets; the UX is what the user goes through and feels. A UI is therefore one of the biggest influences on UX, but not the only one, because speed, content, reliability and support also count.

    How UI decisions change the UX

    UI decisionEffect on the UXExample
    Clear layout and visual hierarchyUsers find what matters first and spend less effortA large "Book now" button above smaller secondary links
    Consistent navigation and controlsUsers learn once and predict where things areThe menu in the same place on every page
    Immediate feedbackUsers know their action workedA button changes colour when pressed and a message says "Added to cart"
    Affordances and signifiersUsers can tell what is clickableButtons look raised; links are underlined
    Readable text and sufficient contrastUsers read comfortably, including in sunlight or with low visionDark text on a light background at a comfortable size
    Forgiving controlsUsers recover from mistakes without frustrationUndo and a confirmation before deleting
    Cluttered screens and tiny targetsErrors, delay and abandonmentFive competing banners around a checkout

    UI design principles

    • Consistency: use the same colours, wording, icons and layout for the same things, both within your system and following common conventions (a magnifying glass means search).
    • Visual hierarchy: use size, weight, colour, contrast, position and white space to show what matters most. People scan pages, so the first things noticed should be the important ones.
    • Feedback: respond to every action within a moment: a pressed state, a spinner, a success or error message.
    • Affordance and signifiers: an affordance is what an object allows you to do; a signifier is the visible clue that tells you (a raised button, a text cursor). Design so that the clue is obvious.
    • Simplicity: remove what does not help the task. Hick's law observes that decision time grows with the number of choices, so fewer, clearer options are faster.
    • Target size and distance: Fitts's law observes that a target is quicker to hit if it is larger and closer, so make touch targets comfortably large, especially on phones.
    • Error prevention and recovery: design so mistakes are unlikely, and make recovery easy.
    • Accessibility: design for the widest range of users from the start.

    Accessibility: WCAG 2.2

    The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) publishes the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, a W3C Recommendation released in October 2023. It sets testable success criteria at three levels (A, AA and AAA) and is organised under four principles, remembered as POUR. Level AA is the usual target for websites.

    PrincipleMeaningExamples of what to do
    PerceivableUsers can perceive the content with the senses they haveText alternatives (alt text) for images; captions for video; do not use colour alone to convey meaning; text contrast of at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text)
    OperableUsers can operate every controlEverything works by keyboard; visible focus; no flashing content; touch targets not too small (WCAG 2.2 adds a minimum target size of 24 by 24 CSS pixels)
    UnderstandableContent and behaviour are predictable and clearPlain language; labelled form fields; helpful error messages; consistent navigation
    RobustContent works with current and future technology, including assistive toolsValid, semantic HTML; correct roles and names for controls

    Accessibility is also a legal matter. The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth) makes discrimination on the basis of disability unlawful in the provision of services, and inaccessible websites can breach it. WCAG is the accepted technical measure. Accessible design also helps many people without disability: captions in noisy places, high contrast in bright light, and keyboard shortcuts for speed.

    Scenario A local pool's booking page uses pale grey text on white, a "Book" icon with no label, and a date picker that can only be operated with a mouse. Fixes: darken the text to meet the 4.5:1 contrast ratio, add a text label to the icon, use a standard date input that also works with the keyboard, and provide visible focus outlines. Each change improves the UX for everyone and removes a barrier for some.
    HSC exam focus "Explain how the UI impacts the UX" wants cause and effect: name a UI feature, say what the user experiences as a result, and say why. For example, "consistent navigation lets users predict where to find things, which reduces errors and effort, so the experience is more efficient". Keep the two terms distinct: UI is the interface, UX is the overall experience.

    Design Thinking for a Front-End System

    🎯 (EC-11-08, EC-11-09, EC-11-11)

    📌 Apply design thinking to develop a front-end, web-based interactive media system incorporating UX and UI principles

    NESA's glossary describes design thinking as a process where a need or opportunity is identified and a design solution is developed, with economic, environmental and social impacts core to it. It can be used to understand a problem, generate ideas and refine a design based on evaluation and testing. The stages below (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test) are applied here to a front-end web-based system: the part of a web system that users see and use, built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript. The running example is a lunch pre-ordering website for a school canteen.

    1. Empathise

    Understand the people you are designing for. Interview students, staff and canteen volunteers, observe the lunch queue, and survey people about how they currently order. Build a short persona (a fictional but evidence-based user: for example "Sam, Year 8, orders on a phone on the bus") and an empathy map (what they say, think, do and feel). Include users with different abilities and devices, for example a student who uses a screen reader. Outputs: notes, quotes and observed pain points (for example, "the queue is too long and I miss out on my order").

    2. Define

    Turn what you learned into a clear problem statement and measurable goals. Example: "Students need a fast, easy way to order lunch before school so they can avoid queues, and volunteers need orders arranged in advance." Add success criteria you can test: "A new user can place an order in under a minute without help", and "The site meets WCAG 2.2 level AA". Output: problem statement, requirements and success criteria.

    3. Ideate

    Generate many possible solutions before choosing one. Use brainstorming, sketching several layouts quickly, and mapping the user's journey. Then choose ideas using criteria such as desirable (users want it), feasible (we can build it) and viable (it is sustainable within the time and budget). Output: a chosen concept, a site map and a storyboard of the main path (see design tools).

    4. Prototype

    Build a cheap, quick model to test. Start with paper or wireframes, move to a clickable mock-up, and for a front-end web system build a working page in HTML, CSS and JavaScript. A prototype can be incomplete because its purpose is learning. Incorporate UX and UI principles from the start: semantic HTML (headings, labels, buttons), consistent layout, a clear hierarchy, immediate feedback, adequate contrast and keyboard access. A small front-end prototype of the ordering screen:

    <main>

    Watch real users try the prototype. Give each a realistic task ("Order a chicken wrap for first break"), ask them to think aloud, and observe without helping. Record task success, time taken, errors and comments. Five users will usually reveal most serious problems. Then decide what to change. This might mean fixing a label, redesigning a screen, or going back to Define if you discover you solved the wrong problem. Repeat until the success criteria are met, then evaluate against the requirements and accessibility guidelines.

    Interactive Data Journalism

    🎯 (EC-11-04, EC-11-05, EC-11-11)

    📌 Develop and publish an interactive work of data journalism

    What is data journalism?

    Data journalism finds, analyses and presents data to tell a news story or explain an issue. The story is built from evidence in a dataset, such as government statistics, and is often presented with interactive charts, maps or searchable tables so the audience can explore the evidence for themselves. For example, instead of writing "rents rose", an interactive graphic lets each reader look up their own suburb. Interactive data journalism combines skills you already use: research, data handling, visualisation, front-end development and writing.

    Workflow, step by step

    1. Question. Ask something specific and answerable: "Has the share of students who walk or ride to school changed since 2019?" Identify the audience and what they need to know.
    2. Data. Find trustworthy data: national statistics from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (abs.gov.au), the government data portal data.gov.au, or Transport for NSW open data. Record the source, date, licence and definitions. Check whether you may reuse it and how to credit it. Or collect your own data by survey, and record how.
    3. Clean and analyse. Remove duplicates and errors, make units and dates consistent, and handle missing values openly. Then calculate the summaries that answer the question (totals, percentages, change over time) in a spreadsheet or code. Check results against a second source, and ask whether the data could tell a different story.
    4. Visualise. Choose the chart that suits the question: lines for change over time, bars for comparing categories, maps for location. Label axes and units, start bar-chart scales at zero, use a colour scheme that works for colour-blind readers, and add alt text and a data table.
    5. Publish. Build the interactive page, test it on phones and with a keyboard, and publish it to your school's web space or a free static host so your audience can read it. State the source, the date and the method, and give a way to send corrections.

    Ethics of data journalism

    • Accuracy and honesty: report what the data shows. Do not cherry-pick, and do not imply that correlation proves cause.
    • Context and fair scales: a truncated axis or unlabelled units can make small changes look dramatic. Present the numbers in context, such as rates per 1,000 people rather than raw counts when populations differ.
    • Privacy: small groups can be re-identified from data that looks anonymous. Publish only what is needed and never expose personal details.
    • Consent and sources: if you collect data from people, explain the purpose and get consent (and a guardian's if under 18).
    • Bias and representativeness: ask who is missing from the data. A survey of 20 friends does not represent a school.
    • Indigenous data: data about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples raises data sovereignty. NESA describes data sovereignty as the right of Indigenous Peoples to determine the creation, collection, ownership and application of data that is for and about them. Frameworks such as the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) and Maiam nayri Wingara are guides to good practice; involve the communities concerned rather than speaking for them.
    • Accessibility and transparency: make the story usable by everyone, share the method and correct mistakes openly.

    A worked mini-example you can build

    Story: "How do students get to school?" The dataset below is invented for practice and is not real. For a real story you would use a published dataset or your own survey. The interactive page has a slider to choose a year; the bars update, a sentence summarises the biggest mode for screen-reader users, and a table gives the underlying data.

    1. Question: Which way of getting to school is most common, and how has that changed?
    2. Data: percentages for four travel modes in four years, stored in a small JavaScript object (a real project would load a CSV or JSON file and record the source).
    3. Analyse: find the largest value in each year; check that each year sums to 100%.
    4. Visualise: bars of equal thickness with one colour (length carries the data), value labels, and a table.
    5. Publish: save the file as index.html, upload it, and add a source line and a contact for corrections.

    Choosing a Project Management Approach

    🎯 (EC-11-08, EC-11-09)

    📌 Select an appropriate project management approach to develop an interactive media-based solution

    NESA's glossary defines project management as the process of planning, organising, controlling resources, monitoring timelines and activities, and completing a project to achieve a goal that meets identified criteria. The approach you choose sets how the work is organised over time. Three are common for interactive media projects.

    Three approaches

    • Waterfall completes the stages in order (requirements, design, development, testing, installation, maintenance). Each stage is finished and signed off before the next starts, and the client sees a working system near the end. It emphasises planning and documentation.
    • Agile works in short cycles (often one to four weeks, called sprints in Scrum). Each cycle plans, builds, tests and reviews a small working piece, with the client giving feedback each time. Work is prioritised from a backlog, and the plan changes as learning arrives.
    • Prototyping builds a quick, cut-down version early so users can react to it, then refines it (or throws it away and builds properly). It focuses on discovering what users need before committing to a full build. Prototyping is often used inside waterfall or agile projects.

    Search, sort and selection processes via online retail services

    Online retail depends on helping people find the right item among many. The typical path is search, filter, sort, compare and select, then add to cart. Each step reduces the number of choices, which speeds decisions. The flowchart shows the logic, using NESA's flowchart symbols.

    Good retail interaction features include a search box that suggests terms as you type and tolerates spelling mistakes; filters shown as check boxes or sliders (category, size, price); a sort menu; a results count; clear product cards with price and image; and a cart that always shows what is in it. Here is a small working example of search, filter and sort in JavaScript:

    <label for="q">Search</label> <input id="q" type="search">
<label for="cat">Category</label>
<select id="cat"><option value="">All</option><option>Shirts</option><option>Shoes</option><option>Bags</option></select>
<label for="sort">Sort by</label>
<select id="sort"><option value="name">Name (A to Z)</option><option value="low">Price (low to high)</option><option value="high">Price (high to low)</option></select>
<p id="count" role="status"></p>
<ul id="results"></ul>

<script>
  const products = [
    { name: 'Polo shirt', category: 'Shirts', price: 24 },
    { name: 'Sports shirt', category: 'Shirts', price: 19 },
    { name: 'Leather shoes', category: 'Shoes', price: 65 },
    { name: 'Sneakers', category: 'Shoes', price: 48 },
    { name: 'School bag', category: 'Bags', price: 39 }
  ];
  const q = document.getElementById('q'), cat = document.getElementById('cat'), sort = document.getElementById('sort');

  function show() {
    let list = products
      .filter(p => p.name.toLowerCase().includes(q.value.trim().toLowerCase()))   // search
      .filter(p => !cat.value || p.category === cat.value);                        // filter
    if (sort.value === 'name') list.sort((a, b) => a.name.localeCompare(b.name));  // sort
    if (sort.value === 'low') list.sort((a, b) => a.price - b.price);
    if (sort.value === 'high') list.sort((a, b) => b.price - a.price);
    document.getElementById('count').textContent = list.length + ' items found';
    document.getElementById('results').innerHTML = list.map(p => `<li>${p.name}: $${p.price}</li>`).join('')
      || '<li>No matches. Try a different word or clear the filters.</li>';
  }
  [q, cat, sort].forEach(el => el.addEventListener('input', show));
  show();
</script>

    Notice that every control has a visible <label>, the item count is announced (role="status"), and an empty search tells the user what to do next. Try searching "shirt", choosing a category and sorting by price.

    Ethics of retail interaction Ranking results to promote products that pay the shop the most, or hiding a cheaper option, may steer buyers unfairly. Say when results are sponsored, and let users sort by price.

    Online gaming platforms

    A gaming platform combines a store, a social network and a match-making service. The interaction features that shape the UX include:

    • Onboarding and tutorials: teach the controls in play rather than in a manual, with adjustable difficulty.
    • Lobbies and match-making: match players by skill and connection quality, and show waiting time (visibility of status).
    • Immediate feedback: sound, animation and vibration confirm actions; health and score are always visible.
    • Social features: friends lists, party chat, quick text phrases, and leaderboards that motivate without shaming.
    • Performance indicators: a visible ping or connection warning, because delay damages the experience.
    • Accessibility options: remappable controls, subtitles, colour-blind modes, adjustable text size and reduced-motion settings.
    • Safety and control: report and mute buttons, parental controls, spending limits and clear age ratings.
    Scenario A Year 11 group prototypes a "study lobby" for a school gaming club. Players choose a role, see who is waiting, and start when four have joined. They use a countdown and a sound cue (feedback), offer a text-size setting and a mute button (accessibility and safety), and test with a beginner who cannot find the Leave button. Moving that button to the same place as on every other screen (consistency) fixes it.
    HSC exam focus "Apply" means using a feature in a specified system. Choose a context from the question, name a specific interaction feature (search filters, report button, match-making lobby), and state the UX principle it supports and the outcome for the user. A short annotated screen design or flowchart with a clear justification can earn more than a paragraph of description.
