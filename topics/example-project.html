Why this matters now

The hardest part of an enterprise project is rarely the programming. It is knowing what a finished project looks like: what the problem statement should say, how a Gantt chart shows a task that is running late, or what a test table looks like when it is honest about a failure. Most students only see the finished product, never the working.

This page follows one invented project, Canteen Insights, through all four of NESA’s stages and shows the real artefact at each one. The centrepiece is a working dashboard that runs in your browser on made-up canteen sales. Every number on it can be traced to a SQL query and a spreadsheet formula, and you are invited to try to break it.

Try this as you learn Choose one artefact, such as the Gantt chart, the data dictionary or the test table, and ask whether a stranger could use it to check or rebuild the project. If the answer is no, work out what is missing.

Meet Canteen Insights

Start here A fictional Year 12 project, followed from problem to evaluation

This project is fictional

Canteen Insights, the student Jordan, the canteen manager Sandra, the peer tester Priya, Kestrel Ridge High School and every survey result, quote, date and number on this page were invented for these notes. The sales data are generated by a program. Nothing here describes a real school, person or canteen.

Canteen Insights is a sales and stock dashboard for a school canteen. The canteen manager, Sandra, wants to see what sells, when it sells and what to reorder, so that popular items stop running out and fresh food stops being thrown away. Jordan, a Year 12 student, builds it as an HSC enterprise project.

How to use this page

Read it as a model, not a template. Notice what makes each artefact useful to someone who was not there: a named owner, a date, a measurable criterion, an expected result. When you build your own project, ask of every artefact whether a stranger could use it to check or rebuild your work.

Part 1 Identifying and defining

Every project starts with a problem that somebody actually has. In this part Jordan writes the problem down, agrees what the system must and must not do, plans the six weeks, and checks who could be affected.

Ask yourself If Sandra changed her mind in week three, which part of Jordan’s plan would tell you what was now at risk?

The Client and the Problem

Artefact Client profile and problem definition

Problem definition

The client and the setting

Sandra manages the canteen at the fictional Kestrel Ridge High School. She works with a small team of paid staff and parent volunteers, serving about the same set of twelve items every day. Sales are rung up on a till that can export a weekly file of what was sold. Orders to suppliers are placed on Fridays, and fresh food such as pies and rolls is prepared each morning.

At the moment Sandra decides how much to order and prepare from memory and a paper stock sheet that is counted by hand on Friday afternoons. The till export is filed and never looked at.

Problem statement

Problem statement (approved 10 July 2026). The canteen manager cannot easily see which items sell, on which days, or how much fresh food is thrown away, so she orders and prepares by habit. Popular items sometimes sell out and other items are wasted. She needs a simple way to see sales and waste, and to be told what to order next week.

A good problem statement names who has the problem, what is going wrong and what a solution must let them do, without describing the solution. Jordan deliberately did not write “build a dashboard”: a spreadsheet, a printed report or a better paper stock sheet might have solved it too, and choosing between them is part of the project.

Success criteria

Success criteria turn the problem into something that can be checked at the end. Jordan and Sandra agreed these four, and the evaluation comes back to each one.

#CriterionHow it will be measured
1Sandra can read the top seller and the waste percentage for any chosen weeksTimed trial with Sandra: under one minute, with no help
2The dashboard’s totals match the till’s own figuresThree-way check of the dashboard, a SQL query and a spreadsheet on the same records
3Sandra receives a reorder list she trusts and can explainThe rules are written as a decision tree that Sandra approves; each suggestion shows the rule used
4Nothing collected identifies a student or staff memberReview of the till export and the stored fields

Stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone affected by the system. Sandra is the client and the main user. Canteen staff and volunteers count stock and would enter corrections. Students are affected indirectly by what the canteen stocks, but never use the system. The school leadership cares about food waste and cost, and the supplier receives Sandra’s orders. Jordan is the developer, and Priya, another student, agreed to act as a beta tester.

Problem definitionA statement of who has a problem, what is going wrong and what a solution must achieve, written before any solution is chosen.
StakeholderA person or group who is affected by the system or can influence it.
Success criterionA measurable statement that shows whether the project solved the problem.

Requirements specification

Exam guidance

A question that says identify or describe requirements is usually looking for a functional and a non-functional example, each tied to the scenario. “Fast” on its own scores poorly: “a filter change recalculates every panel without a noticeable delay” is testable. Do not confuse a constraint (a limit on the project, such as the budget) with a requirement (a property of the system).

Exam guidance

A Gantt question usually gives a task list with durations and dependencies and asks you to draw or complete the chart. Award yourself marks for a dated time scale, task names, correct start and end positions, dependency arrows, at least one milestone, and, if resources are asked for, a name against every bar. If percentage complete is required, the solid part must match the number.

Social, Ethical and Legal Considerations

Artefact Impact review: privacy, fairness, health, accessibility and data retention

Impact review

Before building anything Jordan asked, for each stakeholder, who could be helped and who could be harmed. The review is short, and each finding changed the design.

AreaConsiderationDecision
PrivacyPersonal information is information about an identifiable individual. A till export can carry names, card details or student numbers if the till is set up to record them.The system only ever imports item, quantity and date. The import step ignores any other column and refuses a file whose headings are not the expected ones. No student or staff details are needed for any requirement, so none are collected (NFR3).
Data retentionData kept forever are a risk and a cost. The Australian Privacy Principles ↗ expect entities to destroy or de-identify personal information they no longer need; sales counts are not personal information, but the habit is worth having.Item-level rows are kept for 12 months, then reduced to weekly totals by item. This is the client’s decision, not a legal requirement, and it is written in the maintenance plan.
FairnessA price rise can hurt families with the least money, and the what-if panel makes price rises look attractive on paper.The panel shows the effect on units sold as well as revenue, and Sandra agreed price changes are for her to decide, not the software.
Health and wellbeingA dashboard that only rewards high revenue could push the menu towards less healthy items.Reorder rules are about quantity, not which items to promote. Menu decisions stay with the school’s healthy-food guidelines.
AccessibilityStaff and volunteers have different vision, motor skills and devices. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2 ↗) set out good practice.Keyboard operation, text alternatives for charts, colour never the only signal, readable at phone width, and reduced-motion settings respected (NFR2).
EmploymentAutomation can worry the people whose jobs it touches.The dashboard supports Sandra’s decisions. It does not replace anyone, and staff helped choose the reorder rules.
Legal and ownershipSoftware, images and fonts have licence terms, and the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) ↗ applies to most Australian organisations and agencies that handle personal information. Government schools may also be covered by NSW privacy law.The dashboard uses no third-party code, images or fonts beyond the browser’s own. Jordan wrote everything, and the school agreed how the finished work is shared.

Notice the pattern: each row ends with a design decision. A social or legal review that produces only a list of worries has not done its job. The most effective privacy control here is not a rule, it is not collecting the data in the first place.

Exam guidance

Questions on social, ethical and legal issues reward answers tied to the scenario. Name the issue (privacy), say why it applies (the export could contain personal details), and give the action taken (the import discards other columns). Avoid quoting legislation you are unsure of: describing the principle accurately, such as “collect only what you need”, is worth more than a misquoted Act.

Part 2 Researching and planning

Now Jordan finds out what is really going on and designs the system on paper. Interviews and surveys supply the facts, and diagrams, tables and a storyboard turn them into a plan that Sandra can check before any code is written.

Ask yourself Which of these artefacts could Sandra, who does not write code, check for mistakes, and which could only Jordan?

Interviews, Surveys and Prototypes

Artefact Research summaries, analytical report extract, prototype log

Requirements research

Jordan used more than one research tool, because each answers a different question. Interviews give depth, surveys give breadth, an analytical report of existing data gives evidence, and prototypes test ideas cheaply.

Interview with the client

A structured interview asks the same planned questions in the same order and records the answers. Jordan interviewed Sandra on Monday 6 July for 30 minutes, with open questions that invited stories rather than yes or no answers.

QuestionSummary of the answer
Walk me through how you decide what to order each week.Looks at last week’s paper stock sheet on Friday, adds a bit “if the weather is cold”, and phones the order through. No sales figures are used.

Which decisions would you most like help with?How many of each fresh item to prepare each day, and what to order for next week.

Surveys

A survey collects the same answers from many people, so results can be counted. Jordan ran two short paper-and-form surveys on Tuesday 7 July and Wednesday 8 July: one of students (to test how common sell-outs are from the customer’s side) and one of canteen staff and volunteers (to find out how fresh-food quantities are decided today). Sample question: “This term, how often have you found the item you wanted sold out at the canteen?” with fixed choices, so answers can be counted. A poor version, “Do you like the canteen?”, would not have led to any decision.

Analytical report from existing data

The most convincing evidence was already in the till export. Jordan loaded eight weeks of it into a spreadsheet and summarised what it showed. This is an extract from the report.

Findings, weeks 1 to 8 (fictional data). Meat pies were sold out (everything prepared was sold) on 8 of 8 Fridays. Overall, 8.0% of the items put out for sale were thrown away, costing $2,151.60 at cost price. Waste is concentrated in fresh food: ham salad rolls 22.7%, toasties 19.9%, fruit cups 19.6% and sausage rolls 17.1%, against 9.0% for pies.

Recommendation. Prepare more pies on Fridays and fewer on Mondays, and cut the preparation of rolls, toasties and fruit cups. Test the changes in a pilot before applying them to every item.

Limitation. The till records what was sold, not what customers wanted. On the days pies sold out, real demand was higher than the figures show.

Exam guidance

If asked to apply tools to inform requirements, choose a tool to fit the question and give a sample question or output, as above. Compare tools when asked: an interview gives detail from few people, a survey gives numbers from many, and an analytical report shows what people did rather than what they say. Always note a limitation, such as a small or self-selected sample.

Modelling the System: DFDs and the System Flowchart

Artefact Level 0 and Level 1 data flow diagrams, system flowchart, informatics table

System models

Jordan drew three models. The data flow diagrams show what data moves, the system flowchart shows which devices and processes carry it, and a table of informatics names the participants, data and components. All use NESA’s notation.

Level 0 data flow diagram

The Level 0 diagram (or context diagram) shows the whole system as one process, with every external entity and no data stores. It is the picture Sandra checked first, because she could see what goes in and what comes out without any technical detail.

Level 1 data flow diagram

Level 1 opens the circle: four processes, each a job the system does, and two data stores. The data stores are the database tables from the schema. Every Level 0 flow appears here again with the same label, which is how you check the two levels balance.

Common mistakes with DFDs

Putting data stores on a Level 0 diagram; joining two entities or two stores directly; leaving arrows unlabelled; labelling a flow with an action (“process”) rather than the data; and forgetting that a Level 1 diagram must show every Level 0 flow.

System flowchart

A system flowchart shows the main processes and devices, and the arrows show the direction of data. It answers a different question from the DFD: what hardware and storage does the system touch? This one uses eight of NESA’s nine symbols; nothing in Canteen Insights needs magnetic tape.

Informatics: participants, data and components

Informatics is the science of processing data for storage and retrieval. To clarify each of the relevant informatics in the new system, Jordan listed who takes part, what data exist, and which components handle them at each step.

Exam guidance

2025 HSC candidates were asked to compare Level 0 and Level 1 diagrams using the correct symbols. Marks come from things a marker can see: a circle for a process, an open-ended rectangle for a store, a closed rectangle for an entity, labelled curved arrows with heads, one process and no stores at Level 0, several processes and at least one store at Level 1. Add one sentence saying what each level is for.

Reorder Rules: the Decision Tree

Artefact Decision tree that the dashboard runs

Decision tree

The rules for reordering had to be ones Sandra understood and could challenge, so Jordan drew them as a decision tree before writing any code. A decision tree represents all possible combinations of decisions and their resulting actions, and every path ends in a final action.

The tree is the code

This diagram is not a picture of the rules. It is the rules. The function that decides the action in the dashboard’s code has one branch for each path, in the same order:

function decide(perishable, wastePct, daysCover) {
  if (perishable) return wastePct > 10 ? 'forecast' : 'buffer';
  if (daysCover < 3) return 'now';
  if (daysCover < 5) return 'soon';
  return 'hold';
}

Two other parts of the reorder list follow from the tree. The forecast is the average units sold per day over the last ten trading days, times five days. Order forecast + 10% adds a buffer so fresh items do not sell out, and Order to forecast leaves the buffer off where waste is high. Reorder now and Reorder soon order enough for five days of sales plus a 20% buffer, less what is on hand. In the dashboard, the list shows which rule each suggestion used, so Sandra can disagree with a specific step.

Item (in the sample data)Path through the treeAction
Bottled water: 58 on hand, about 36 a dayNot perishable, 1.6 days left (under 3)Reorder now
Flavoured milk: 70 on hand, about 19 a dayNot perishable, 3.7 days left (3 or more, under 5)Reorder soon
Popcorn: 95 on hand, about 18 a dayNot perishable, 5.3 days left (5 or more)Hold
Ham salad roll: waste last week 16.2%Perishable, waste over 10%Order to forecast

Exam guidance

NESA draws decision trees as rectangles joined by labelled branches (Course Specifications p.8). A frequent error is drawing diamonds, which belong to flowcharts. Check that branches from one question cover every case exactly once, so a value such as exactly 3.0 days cannot fit two branches. When asked to test a tree, choose data on both sides of every threshold as well as on it.

Schema

The schema shows the structure: which tables exist, which field is each table’s primary key, and how tables are linked. Jordan normalised the data: an item’s name, price and category are stored once in Items, not repeated in each of the 480 sales rows. If a price changes, one row changes, and the data cannot contradict themselves.

Exam guidance

A data dictionary question expects all six NESA columns and sensible formats: a fixed-length code such as XNNNN is checked more easily than free text. For a schema, mark every primary key, every foreign key and the 1 and infinity ends of each relationship. When asked to explain normalisation, say that duplicated data can be updated inconsistently, and give an example from the scenario.

Computational, Design and Systems Thinking

Artefact Three ways of thinking, applied to the project

Thinking skills

The syllabus asks how three kinds of thinking help in designing and developing a system. Jordan used all three, at different moments, on the same problem.

ThinkingWhat it meansWhere it appears in Canteen Insights
Computational thinkingAnalysing a problem so that a person or a computer can solve it: organising data logically, breaking a problem into parts, interpreting patterns and designing algorithms.Decomposition: the dashboard is filters, KPIs, charts, pivot, what-if, reorder and data entry, each built and tested alone. Pattern recognition: Fridays are busiest and pies always sell out. Abstraction: a sale is just item, day and quantity; who bought it is deliberately ignored. Algorithm: the reorder rules.

SQL and Spreadsheet Formulas Behind the Numbers

Artefact Queries and formulas that produce the dashboard’s figures, checked live

Queries and formulas

Jordan’s test for “are the totals right?” was to get the same numbers by two other routes: a SQL query, and spreadsheet formulas. The queries use only the four keywords in NESA’s Course Specifications, SELECT, FROM, WHERE and ORDER BY, and tables are joined by matching key fields in the WHERE clause. Because those keywords select and sort rows but do not add them up, the totals come from adding a column of the result, exactly as you would in a spreadsheet.

Query A: one item, one week

“Show every sales row for meat pies in week 5, with the price.” The result has five rows (Monday to Friday). Adding Sold gives 215 pies, and Sold × UnitPrice gives $967.50.

SELECT DailySales.SaleDate, Items.ItemName, DailySales.Prepared, DailySales.Sold, DailySales.Wasted, Items.UnitPrice
FROM DailySales, Items
WHERE Items.ItemID = DailySales.ItemID
AND DailySales.SaleDate >= '01/06/2026'
AND DailySales.SaleDate <= '05/06/2026'
AND Items.ItemName = 'Meat pie'
ORDER BY DailySales.SaleDate ASC, Items.ItemName ASC

Checking in your browser…

Query B: a category over several weeks

“Show every drinks sale from week 3 to week 5.” This one joins three tables. Each extra table needs its own line of key equality in WHERE.

SELECT DailySales.SaleDate, Items.ItemName, DailySales.Prepared, DailySales.Sold, DailySales.Wasted, Items.UnitPrice
FROM DailySales, Items, Categories
WHERE Items.ItemID = DailySales.ItemID
AND Categories.CategoryID = Items.CategoryID
AND DailySales.SaleDate >= '18/05/2026'
AND DailySales.SaleDate <= '05/06/2026'
AND Categories.CategoryName = 'Drinks'
ORDER BY DailySales.SaleDate ASC, Items.ItemName ASC

Query C: where is the waste?

“List the days on which ten or more units of a perishable item were thrown out, worst first.” The comparison operators and the sorted output do the analysis. Press the button to run this exact text in the browser and compare the count with a plain JavaScript filter over the same records.

SELECT DailySales.SaleDate, Items.ItemName, DailySales.Prepared, DailySales.Wasted
FROM DailySales, Items
WHERE Items.ItemID = DailySales.ItemID
AND Items.Perishable = 'Y'
AND DailySales.Wasted >= 10
ORDER BY DailySales.Wasted DESC, DailySales.SaleDate ASC

The same numbers in a spreadsheet

In a spreadsheet the sales rows go on a Sales sheet and the item list on an Items sheet. The formulas use only functions from NESA’s list: SUM, MAXIMUM, IF and LOOKUP. LOOKUP needs the ItemID column to be sorted ascending, which it is (I01 to I12). References to the Items sheet are absolute ($A$2:$A$13) so they stay fixed when the formula is filled down; references to the current row are relative.

CellFormulaWhat it does
Sales!F2 (Revenue)=D2*LOOKUP(B2,Items!$A$2:$A$13,Items!$D$2:$D$13)Sold times the price found by looking up the ItemID
Sales!G2 (In range?)=IF(A2>=Summary!$B$1,IF(A2<=Summary!$B$2,1,0),0)1 if the date is inside the chosen range, otherwise 0
Sales!H2 (Revenue counted)=F2*G2Revenue for rows in range only
Summary!B4 (Revenue)=SUM(Sales!H2:H481)The revenue tile
Summary!B5 (Items sold)=SUM(Sales!I2:I481)The items-sold tile (column I is =D2*G2)

Spreadsheet software names these functions differently (MAXIMUM is written MAX in most packages), so in the exam use NESA’s wording.

Going beyond the syllabus

Real SQL can add up values for you with SUM and GROUP BY, which would return the totals directly. Those are not in NESA’s list of keywords, so an HSC answer should not depend on them. They are worth knowing about, and a database used in the real world would use them.

Exam guidance

2025 HSC candidates were asked to write a query using four SQL keywords and a join. Full marks need SELECT with the right fields, FROM with every table, WHERE with both the search condition and the key equality that joins the tables, and ORDER BY with ASC or DESC. Missing the join condition is the most common lost mark: without it every row of one table is paired with every row of the other.

Limitations

  • The till records what was sold, not what customers wanted. On the days pies sold out, demand is understated, so the forecast for pies is probably too low.
  • The what-if panel uses assumed price sensitivities. They came from interviews and are not measured, so its projections show direction, not exact amounts.
  • Only eight weeks of data and a single canteen. Seasonal effects, such as cold weather and end-of-term days, are not modelled.
  • The import is manual, so a busy week without an import leaves the dashboard out of date.
  • Accessibility was tested in two browsers and by keyboard, not with screen-reader users.

Impact of the system on its environments

An enterprise system does not only work or fail technically. It changes the places it is used in.

Modifying the design

Feedback led to three changes, each written up in the process diary and the change log: the four screens were merged into one page (P3), the what-if panel now shows waste as well as revenue (P4), and the evaluation now states that sold-out days hide real demand. The next version would add a column for “sold out before closing” to the till import, so the system can see lost sales.

Exam guidance

Evaluate means a judgement supported by criteria and evidence, with strengths and weaknesses. A high-scoring answer states each criterion, cites a result, and reaches a conclusion (“mostly met”), then names a limitation and an improvement. Do not evaluate against features you added later, only against the original success criteria.

Trialling the documentation

Documentation is only useful if someone else can follow it. Jordan gave Priya the maintenance guide and asked her to add a new item, an iced tea, with no help. This is the extract she followed.

  1. Open the Items table and add a new row at the bottom.
  2. Give it the next ItemID in order, for example I13, using the format XNN.
  3. Enter the name, choose its CategoryID from the Categories table, and enter the price and cost in dollars.
  4. Enter Y for Perishable if unsold units are thrown out, otherwise N, and enter the units on hand.
  5. Check the dashboard: the item appears in the item list, and the three-way check still agrees.

Priya stopped at step 3 because it was not clear whether the cost meant the cost of one item or a box. Jordan rewrote the step to say “cost of one item, for example $0.80”, and the change is in the log. Trials find problems that the author never sees, which is why NESA lists trialling the operation and maintenance documentation as part of verifying a system.

Exam guidance

Maintenance questions ask what must be done after implementation. Give specific, scheduled tasks with an owner (a weekly backup, a termly review of thresholds), not general advice such as “keep it updated”. Documentation belongs in the answer: a user guide for operators and a maintenance guide for whoever changes the system.

Process diary

A process diary documents the progress of a project. NESA lists what each entry should include: the date, the person making the entry, progress since the last entry, tasks achieved, stumbling blocks and how they were managed, possible approaches for upcoming tasks, reflective comments and resources used. Here are two of Jordan’s entries (fictional).

Jordan
Progress

Notice that the second entry connects to the Gantt chart, the test table and the version log. A diary earns its place because it is the link between the plan, the problems and the decisions. This is what NESA means by ongoing evaluation: judging progress as it happens, not only at the end.

Exam guidance

If asked to write or complete a process diary entry, include all eight items NESA lists. The most-missed are the stumbling block and how it was managed, and a reflective comment that shows something was learned. Specific details, such as the task number, a percentage or the number of hours, make an entry credible.
