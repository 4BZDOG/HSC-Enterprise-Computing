|---|---|---|

"Indicative hours" are NESA's wording for the time allocation of each focus area; they are indicative, not mandatory.

## 2. Project requirements

### 2.1 What the syllabus documents say

- **Year 11:** practical experiences and project work are described as "an essential component of the Enterprise Computing Stage 6 course" [TA, Year 11 focus areas]. NESA's Year 11 project advice is illustrative ("Potential projects include: …") and gives teachers "flexibility to plan projects with students that best address their individual needs and interests, as well as the context and resourcing of the school" [TA].
- **Year 12 Enterprise Project (30 indicative hours):** NESA teaching advice [TA, Year 12 – Enterprise project]: "The Enterprise Project is to be completed in Year 12 and will require students to develop a project of their choice based on the knowledge and skills developed during the course. Students have an opportunity to develop deeper knowledge, understanding and skills in an area of interest as they engage in an Enterprise Project that addresses an identified real-world problem. Students refine and apply project management skills as they develop, document, implement and evaluate their project solution. Learning in this focus area emphasises project management and development using digital design tools and explores the concept of delivering solutions for a client. Teachers are encouraged to integrate the learning content of this focus area within all other Year 12 focus areas."
- The Year 12 focus area is organised around the **design and production process**: identifying and defining; researching and planning; producing and implementing; testing and evaluating [TA; also the syllabus content subheadings]. Students "individually or collaboratively develop, document, implement and evaluate their project solution".
- Project documentation tools named by the syllabus and Course Specifications: problem definition, time and resource management including Gantt charts, an iterative approach, process diary (log book), budget, system flowcharts, data flow diagrams, decision trees, storyboards, prototypes, interviews/surveys and testing/evaluation (see syllabus content, "Year 12 – Enterprise project", and [SPECS] pages 4–13). The process diary content is specified in [SPECS] page 13.
- Example projects NESA lists: creating a program that communicates wirelessly with other devices; creating, executing and debugging a Python program; a prototype lighting system driven by sensor readings; a step-counter used to collect and analyse data over seven days [TA].
- NESA states that the learning experiences "may highlight opportunities for students to incorporate data science into their Enterprise Computing project" and the same for data visualisation [TA].

### 2.2 What is NOT specified (checked; not found)

- NESA does **not** publish a mandatory project task, project weighting, minimum/maximum project length, word limit, or submission requirement for Enterprise Computing school-based assessment on the pages checked ([SBA], [HSC-EXAM], [TA], [SYL-CONTENT]). The Enterprise Project is a course focus area with 30 indicative hours; how it is assessed (if at all as a formal task) is a school decision within the rules in section 3.
- There is **no HSC practical examination or submitted major work** for Enterprise Computing: [HSC-EXAM] describes a single written online examination only (section 4). Enterprise Computing does not appear in the list of courses exempted from the take-home task limit (section 3.3) because it has no major project or HSC practical exam.
- NESA's Year 12 sample unit "Enterprise Computing Year 12 – Enterprise computing project" (see `ec-official-links.md`) is a teaching-and-learning example; it is not an assessment requirement.

## 3. School-based assessment requirements

### 3.1 Year 11 (Preliminary) [SBA]

- Grades: "Schools are required to submit to NESA a grade for each student based on their achievement at the end of the course. Teachers use professional, on-balance judgement to allocate grades based on the Common Grade Scale for Preliminary courses."
- **Mandatory components and weightings:** "The course components and component weightings for Year 11 are mandatory."

| Course component | Weighting % |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and understanding of course content | 50 |
| Knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content | 50 |

- Number, type and weighting of tasks: "Schools have authority to determine the number, type and weighting of assessment tasks, subject to a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15%." NESA therefore does **not** fix a number of tasks for Year 11.
- Guidance (not mandatory): schools should "use a variety of assessment tasks across the year" and "give individual assessment tasks a weighting between 10% and 40%."

### 3.2 Year 12 (HSC school-based assessment) [SBA]

- "NESA requires schools to submit a school-based assessment mark for each Year 12 student who completes this course. Formal school-based assessment tasks should reflect the syllabus outcomes and content. The mark submitted by the school provides a summation of each student's achievement measured at several points throughout the course."
- **Mandatory components and weightings:** "The course components and component weightings for Year 12 are mandatory."

| Course component | Weighting % |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and understanding of course content | 50 |
| Knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content | 50 |

- Number, type and weighting of tasks: "Schools have authority to determine the number, type and weighting of assessment tasks, subject to a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15%."
- Guidance: the same two bullets as Year 11 (variety of tasks; individual task weighting between 10% and 40%).
- The school-based mark is a moderated component of the HSC mark (NESA moderates school-based marks against the external examination; see NESA's Stage 6 assessment pages linked from [SBA]). NESA's EC pages checked do not state the HSC exam/school-based split as a percentage. Under standard NESA practice the HSC mark for a Board Developed course is an equal average of the school-based assessment mark and the examination mark, but that statement is **not** found on the Enterprise Computing pages and is not verified here; confirm in the NESA "HSC: Assessment and reporting" advice before publishing it as a fact.

### 3.3 The take-home task limit (new in Term 4 2026)

- [SBA] states the limit "one take-home task worth no more than 15%" for both years.
- [NOTICE-29] (NESA 29/26, 31 August 2026): "For Preliminary and HSC courses, school-based assessment programs can have no more than one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum weighting of 15%. Take-home assessments are tasks completed outside a classroom, formal examination setting or teacher-approved monitoring conditions. Schools still decide the number, type and weighting of all in-school assessment tasks." Start dates: "For HSC courses, the limit starts from Term 4, 2026. For Preliminary courses, the limit starts from Term 1, 2027."
- [NOTICE-29] exempt courses: "The limit does not apply to courses with a major project or an HSC practical exam." Listed exempt courses: Aboriginal Studies, Society and Culture, Design and Technology, Industrial Technology, Textiles and Design, Visual Arts, Dance, Drama, Music 1, Music 2, English Extension 2, Science Extension, History Extension, Music Extension. **Enterprise Computing is not on the list**, so the limit applies to it. (This is a reading of the published list, not a statement NESA makes specifically about Enterprise Computing.)
- [NOTICE-34] (NESA 34/26, 16 September 2026) confirms changes to ACE rules 2.1.1 and 2.1.2 (and others) "Effective: Immediately", applying to Year 12 students commencing HSC courses from Term 4, 2026 and Year 11 students commencing Preliminary courses from Term 1, 2027.
- Generative AI: [ACE] requires that "Schools must design formal assessment tasks to ensure marks are reflective of a student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and not the use of generative artificial intelligence." [NOTICE-29] adds that schools "should not rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice."

### 3.4 Other assessment-program rules (ACE) [ACE]

- Schools must give students written notice, "typically 14 calendar days", of an upcoming assessment task, including the task weighting, syllabus outcomes assessed, task type, date/time, and marking criteria where appropriate [ACE].
- At the start of each course schools must provide the formal assessment schedule showing outcomes assessed, components and weightings per task, and when tasks are attempted or submitted [ACE].
- Schools must have policies on absence, late submission, illness/misadventure, invalid or unreliable tasks, and appeals [ACE].

## 4. HSC examination specifications

Verbatim from [HSC-EXAM] ("Enterprise Computing HSC examination specifications"):

- "The examination will be worth 80 marks."
- "It will be undertaken by students using a computer."
- "The time allowed is 2 hours and 30 minutes including 10 minutes reading time."
- "Questions may contain stimulus material."
- "Headphones will be required for questions with video and audio stimulus."
- "The examination will consist of two types of test item."

| Item type | Approximate marks | Details (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective-response items** | approx. 20 marks | "Objective-response items may share stimulus with other objective-response items or short-answer items." "Each item will be worth 1 to 4 marks." "There will be 14 to 18 items in total." |
| **Short-answer items** | approx. 60 marks | "Short-answer items may share stimulus with other short-answer items or objective-response items." "At least three items will be worth 4 to 8 marks." "There will be 16 to 18 items in total." |

Note that NESA's specification describes **two item types, not "Section I / Section II"**; the specification does not describe the paper as sectioned. NESA's page also states:

- "The external HSC examination measures student achievement in a range of syllabus outcomes." The examination relates to the syllabus by "providing clear links to syllabus outcomes", "enabling students to demonstrate the levels of achievement outlined in the Performance Band Descriptions", "applying marking guidelines based on criteria that relate to the quality of the response" and "aligning performance in the examination each year to the standards established for the course."
- "Examination questions may require students to integrate knowledge, understanding and skills developed through studying the course. The Year 11 course is assumed knowledge for the Year 12 course."
- Students with disability may be eligible for HSC exam provisions: https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/hsc/exam-provisions

### 4.3 Observed structure of the 2025 HSC exam (from the 2025 marking guidelines PDF)

This is an observation of one exam, not a NESA specification of future papers.
- The marking guidelines contain **Questions 1 to 25**, several with parts (a)/(b).
- Questions 1–10 are single-mark items ("Identifies the correct answer", 1 mark), consistent with objective-response items. Questions 11–13 have criteria worth up to 2–3 marks and are probably multi-part objective items, but the guidelines do not label items by type.
- NESA's marker feedback groups **Questions 14–25** under "Short-answer items". Mark ranges seen in the guidelines include 3-mark, 4-mark, 5-mark and 8-mark responses; **Question 25 is worth 8 marks** (an extended response on how intelligent systems assist structured, semi-structured and unstructured decision-making, supported by stimulus examples), and Question 21 (Level 0 and Level 1 DFD) is worth up to 5 marks.
- Topics assessed in the 2025 short-answer questions (per marker feedback): quantitative and qualitative data (Q14); SQL with a join (Q15b); sensors and expert systems in manufacturing/Industry 4.0 (Q16); data warehousing, data mining and hardware advances (Q17); spreadsheet formulas, IF and lookups with test data (Q18); interface/screen design with form controls (Q19); conditional formatting, pivot tables and chart choice (Q20); DFD Level 0 versus Level 1 (Q21); project management tools and cloud security methods (Q22); dashboards, OLAP, cognitive load (Q23); offshore development and freelance work (Q24); decision support systems and intelligent systems (Q25).
- Reading the feedback across questions: questions draw on Year 11 and Year 12 content (assumed knowledge) and on the Course Specifications notations (DFD, flowcharts, SQL, spreadsheet functions, Gantt charts).

Full marker feedback is on [EXAM-2025]; NESA's own wording is preferred over this summary.

## 5. Key words and command verbs

- The NESA **syllabus glossary** [GLOSS] is the only glossary attached to Enterprise Computing. Its assessment-relevant entries (verbatim):
  - **evaluate**: "Make a judgement based on criteria; determine the value of."
  - **critically analyse/evaluate**: "Add a degree or level of accuracy, depth, knowledge and understanding, logic, questioning, reflection and quality to (analyse/evaluate)."
  - **calculate**: "Ascertain/determine from given facts, figures or information."
  - **clarify**, **classify**, **collaborate**, **producing** are also defined.
  All 81 glossary terms are reproduced in `ec-syllabus-glossary.md`.
- NESA's separate cross-course "Glossary of key words" (analyse, assess, compare, describe, discuss, explain, identify, outline, etc.) is **not linked** from the Enterprise Computing pages checked, and it is not tagged to this syllabus in the glossary data. Command verbs other than those above (for example "outline", "explain", "describe", "discuss") are therefore not officially defined for Enterprise Computing on the pages checked. Do not attribute NESA definitions of them to this course without confirming a source.
- The outcomes use the verbs "describes/explains/applies/explores/analyses/evaluates" (Year 11 versus Year 12 progression) – see `nesa-syllabus-content.md`.
